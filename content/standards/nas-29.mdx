## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Imagine if the price of everything doubled every year — a cup of tea that costs NPR 30 today would cost NPR 60 next year and NPR 120 the year after. When prices spiral this fast, financial statements become meaningless because numbers from last year and this year cannot be compared. NAS 29 provides rules for restating financial statements to account for such extreme inflation.

The good news: **Nepal does not currently experience hyperinflation.** Nepal's inflation rate has historically been between 5% and 8%, far below the roughly 100% cumulative rate over three years that triggers this standard. However, NAS 29 exists in Nepal's accounting standards suite because NFRS adopts the full set of IFRS standards, and students must understand it for exams.

Think of NAS 29 as an "emergency kit" — you hope you never need it, but it's important to know how it works. If Nepal ever faced extreme economic instability, this standard would ensure financial statements remain meaningful by adjusting all numbers for changing prices.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 29 requires financial statements of entities whose functional currency is that of a hyperinflationary economy to be restated in terms of the measuring unit current at the end of the reporting period. This means historical cost figures are adjusted using a general price index so that all amounts are expressed in current purchasing power.

Hyperinflation indicators include: cumulative inflation approaching or exceeding 100% over three years, prices quoted in a stable foreign currency, interest rates and wages linked to a price index, and a general population preference for holding non-monetary assets or stable foreign currency. These are qualitative and quantitative indicators — the 100% threshold is a guideline, not an absolute rule.

While **not currently applicable in Nepal** (CPI inflation has been 4–8% in recent years), the standard is part of the complete NFRS suite adopted on 1 Shrawan 2075 BS. It remains examinable in ACCA SBR and CA Nepal examinations, typically as a theoretical question or in the context of a Nepali entity with a foreign subsidiary operating in a hyperinflationary economy (e.g., a subsidiary in Zimbabwe or Argentina).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 29 implements a current purchasing power (CPP) approach to financial reporting, as codified in <ParaRef std="IAS 29" para="11–28" />. The standard does not prescribe a specific price index but requires a general price index reflecting changes in general purchasing power. The restatement mechanics differ for monetary and non-monetary items: monetary items (cash, receivables, payables) are already expressed in current units and require no restatement; non-monetary items carried at historical cost are restated by applying the change in the general price index from acquisition date to reporting date; non-monetary items carried at revalued amounts are stated at their revaluation-date amounts.

The gain or loss on the net monetary position (<ParaRef std="IAS 29" para="27–28" />) is the mathematical consequence of restating non-monetary items and equity while leaving monetary items unadjusted. This is included in profit or loss and separately disclosed. The standard's interaction with NAS 21 is critical: when translating a hyperinflationary subsidiary's financial statements, the entity first restates under NAS 29 (current purchasing power), then translates using the closing rate for all items (<ParaRef std="IAS 29" para="36" />). This avoids the distortion of translating historical cost figures from a hyperinflationary currency.

For Nepal, the standard's relevance is largely theoretical but not entirely academic. Nepali conglomerates with investments in high-inflation economies (certain African or South American markets) may need to apply NAS 29 to those operations. Additionally, understanding the standard illuminates the limitations of historical cost accounting even in moderate-inflation environments — Nepal's 5–8% annual inflation does erode comparability of long-lived asset values over decades.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope & key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 29 applies when a country's currency is losing value extremely fast — what economists call **hyperinflation**. This is NOT normal inflation like Nepal's 5-8% annual rate.

**Key signs of hyperinflation:**
- Prices roughly double (or more) in three years
- People prefer to keep their savings in gold, property, or foreign currency instead of local money
- Shops change prices daily or weekly
- Wages are linked to a price index

**Key terms:**
- **Hyperinflation** — Extreme, rapid, and uncontrollable price increases in an economy.
- **Restatement** — Adjusting financial statement numbers so they reflect current purchasing power.
- **General price index** — A measure of how much prices have risen overall (like Nepal's Consumer Price Index).
- **Monetary items** — Things like cash, bank balances, and receivables that are already in today's money.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The standard applies to the financial statements (including consolidated statements) of any entity whose functional currency is the currency of a hyperinflationary economy (<ParaRef std="IAS 29" para="1" />). It does not establish an absolute threshold but provides indicators including cumulative three-year inflation approaching or exceeding 100%.

Key definitions:
- **Monetary items** — money held and items to be received or paid in money (cash, trade receivables, loan payables). Already stated in current purchasing power — no restatement needed.
- **Non-monetary items** — all other items (PPE, inventory at cost, equity, intangible assets). Must be restated from their historical date to reporting-date purchasing power.
- **General price index** — an officially published index reflecting changes in general purchasing power. In Nepal, this would be the CPI published by Nepal Rastra Bank.
- **Net monetary position** — monetary assets minus monetary liabilities. Holding net monetary assets during hyperinflation results in a purchasing power loss; holding net monetary liabilities results in a gain.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The standard's scope is triggered by the functional currency assessment under NAS 21 — if the functional currency is hyperinflationary, NAS 29 applies regardless of the entity's presentation currency or parent's domicile. The determination of hyperinflation is a matter of judgement informed by the indicators in <ParaRef std="IAS 29" para="3" />. The IASB does not maintain an official list, but the IPTF (International Practices Task Force) of the AICPA publishes guidance that is widely referenced.

Scope interactions: (a) when hyperinflation ceases, the entity uses the restated amounts at the end of the last hyperinflationary period as the basis for historical cost going forward (<ParaRef std="IAS 29" para="38" />), (b) first-time adopters under NFRS 1 may elect deemed cost for assets in formerly hyperinflationary economies, (c) the standard interacts with NAS 21's translation requirements — comparative amounts in a stable presentation currency are those presented in the prior year's financial statements (not restated for subsequent inflation), creating a departure from normal NAS 21 translation mechanics.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

NAS 29 does not introduce new recognition criteria — rather, it modifies how already-recognised items are measured. Non-monetary items carried at historical cost are restated by applying the change in the general price index from the date of acquisition (or contribution, or revaluation) to the end of the reporting period. Items already carried at current amounts (e.g., NRV for inventory, fair value for investment property) are not restated. The gain or loss on the net monetary position is recognised in profit or loss and separately disclosed (<ParaRef std="IAS 29" para="27–28" />).

## Measurement

**Restatement of the balance sheet:** Non-monetary items at historical cost are restated using the change in the general price index from transaction date to reporting date. Non-monetary items at revalued amounts are stated at the revaluation-date values. Monetary items require no restatement. Equity components (share capital, reserves) are restated from their contribution or origination dates (<ParaRef std="IAS 29" para="14–18" />).

**Restatement of profit or loss:** All income and expense items are restated by applying the change in the general price index from the dates of the transactions to the reporting date. In practice, monthly averages may be used if transactions occur evenly. Depreciation is restated consistently with the restated asset values. The gain or loss on the net monetary position captures the effect of inflation on monetary items and is included in profit or loss (<ParaRef std="IAS 29" para="26" />).

## Presentation & disclosure

Financial statements restated under NAS 29 must clearly disclose: the fact of restatement, the identity and level of the price index at the reporting date and at the end of the previous period, and whether financial statements are based on a historical cost or current cost approach (<ParaRef std="IAS 29" para="39" />). Corresponding prior-period figures are also restated to current measuring units. When the subsidiary of a Nepali parent operates in a hyperinflationary economy, the consolidated financial statements must disclose the basis of translation and the impact on group results.
