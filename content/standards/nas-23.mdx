## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

When a business borrows money to build something that takes a long time — like a hydropower plant, a hotel, or a new factory — it pays interest on that loan during the construction period. NAS 23 says that this interest should be added to the cost of the asset being built, rather than treated as an expense in the income statement.

Think of it this way: if Himalayan Hydropower borrows NPR 100 crore at 10% interest to build a power plant that takes 5 years to complete, the interest during construction (up to NPR 50 crore over 5 years) becomes part of the power plant's cost — just like the cement, steel, and turbines. This makes sense because without the loan, the plant could not have been built.

**Why does this matter?** If the interest were expensed immediately, the company would show huge losses during construction and higher profits afterwards. By capitalising the interest, the cost is spread over the power plant's useful life through depreciation, giving a more accurate picture.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 23 requires the capitalisation of borrowing costs that are directly attributable to the acquisition, construction, or production of a qualifying asset. A qualifying asset is one that necessarily takes a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended use or sale. All other borrowing costs are recognised as an expense in the period incurred.

In Nepal, this standard has outsized importance due to the prevalence of long-duration construction projects. Hydropower projects (5–10 year construction periods), real estate developments, hotel construction, and cement plant expansion all involve significant borrowing costs. With Nepal's relatively high interest rates — base rates of 8–12% across commercial banks — the capitalised interest component can materially inflate asset values. The standard interacts closely with NAS 16 (PPE) for constructed assets and NAS 38 (Intangibles) for internally developed intangible assets.

NAS 23 is fully aligned with IAS 23 — capitalisation is mandatory, not optional.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The 2007 revision of IAS 23 (effective 2009) eliminated the option to expense all borrowing costs, making capitalisation mandatory for qualifying assets. NAS 23 adopts this revised benchmark treatment. The standard's core mechanism — that borrowing costs form part of the cost of a qualifying asset (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="8" />) — represents a departure from the pure matching principle towards a cost-accumulation model.

For Nepal's hydropower sector, the standard creates material accounting consequences. A 50 MW project with NPR 500 crore in borrowings at 10% over a 7-year construction period capitalises approximately NPR 350 crore in interest (before compounding and any investment income offset). This can represent 25–35% of total project cost. The capitalisation rate for general borrowings (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="14" />) — the weighted average of borrowing costs applicable to general borrowings outstanding — is complex when entities have multiple facilities at different rates (term loans, working capital, bonds, debentures).

The standard requires suspension of capitalisation during extended periods when active development is interrupted (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="20" />). This is particularly relevant in Nepal where project delays are common — land acquisition disputes, environmental clearance delays, and political disruptions can halt construction for months. Determining "active development" versus "administrative delay" requires significant judgement.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope & key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 23 covers:
- **Interest on bank loans** used to build long-term assets
- **Interest on debentures and bonds** raised for construction
- **Other borrowing costs** like bank charges and fees directly related to the borrowing

**Not covered:** Interest on loans for buying inventory that is manufactured in large quantities on a repetitive basis — those assets don't qualify even if they take time to produce.

**Key terms:**
- **Borrowing costs** — interest and other costs that the business incurs in connection with borrowing money.
- **Qualifying asset** — an asset that necessarily takes a long time (usually more than 12 months) to get ready for use or sale. Examples: a hydropower plant, a hotel under construction, real estate being developed.
- **Capitalise** — adding the interest cost to the asset's value on the balance sheet, rather than showing it as an expense.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The standard applies to borrowing costs incurred for qualifying assets. Borrowing costs include interest expense calculated using the effective interest method under NFRS 9, finance charges on NFRS 16 lease liabilities, and exchange differences arising from foreign currency borrowings to the extent they are regarded as an adjustment to interest costs.

Key definitions:
- **Qualifying asset** — an asset that necessarily takes a substantial period to get ready for its intended use or sale (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="5" />). Inventories manufactured in large quantities on a repetitive basis are specifically excluded. Financial assets and inventories manufactured over short periods do not qualify.
- **Specific borrowings** — borrowings taken out specifically for the qualifying asset. Investment income on temporary deployment of these funds is deducted from capitalised costs.
- **General borrowings** — where funds are borrowed generally and used for a qualifying asset. A capitalisation rate (weighted average of borrowing costs on outstanding general borrowings) is applied to expenditures on the asset.
- **Commencement, suspension, and cessation** — capitalisation begins when expenditures are incurred, borrowing costs are incurred, and activities to prepare the asset are underway. It is suspended during extended periods of inactivity and ceases when substantially all activities are complete.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope exclusion for inventories "manufactured in large quantities on a repetitive basis" (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="4" />) is clear, but boundary cases arise in Nepal for long-maturation products — aged cheese, whisky, or timber plantations. These would typically qualify if the substantial period test is met, though practice varies.

The exchange difference component is contentious: exchange differences on foreign currency borrowings are capitalised only "to the extent that they are regarded as an adjustment to interest costs" (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="6(e)" />). The IFRS Interpretations Committee has clarified this refers to exchange differences on the interest portion, not the principal. In Nepal, where USD-denominated project finance is common for hydropower (syndicated loans from development finance institutions), the principal exchange loss on a USD 50 million loan can be enormous — and this is expensed, not capitalised.

The interaction with NFRS 16 adds lease finance charges to capitalisable borrowing costs for right-of-use assets — relevant where a qualifying asset is constructed on leased land. The general borrowings capitalisation rate must exclude specific borrowings from both numerator and denominator (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="14" />).

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

Capitalisation of borrowing costs commences when three conditions are simultaneously met: (a) expenditures for the asset are being incurred, (b) borrowing costs are being incurred, and (c) activities necessary to prepare the asset for its intended use or sale are in progress (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="17" />). Activities include more than physical construction — they include technical and administrative work prior to construction, such as obtaining permits. Capitalisation is suspended during extended periods when active development is interrupted and ceases when substantially all the activities necessary to prepare the qualifying asset are complete (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="22" />).

## Measurement

For specific borrowings, the amount capitalised is the actual borrowing costs incurred minus any investment income from temporary investment of those funds (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="12" />). For general borrowings, the capitalisation rate is the weighted average of the borrowing costs applicable to all general borrowings outstanding during the period. The amount capitalised during any period shall not exceed the total borrowing costs incurred during that period (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="14" />). Borrowing costs that do not meet the capitalisation criteria — or that are incurred after the qualifying asset is substantially complete — are expensed as incurred.

## Presentation & disclosure

The financial statements must disclose the amount of borrowing costs capitalised during the period and the capitalisation rate used to determine the amount of borrowing costs eligible for capitalisation (<ParaRef std="IAS 23" para="26" />). Capitalised borrowing costs form part of the cost of the qualifying asset and are subsequently depreciated or amortised along with the asset. Total interest expense (both capitalised and expensed portions) should be apparent from the disclosures.
