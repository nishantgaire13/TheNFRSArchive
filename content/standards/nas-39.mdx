## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 39 was Nepal's standard for how to **recognise** (record) and **measure** (value) financial instruments — things like bank loans, investments in shares, bonds, derivatives, and trade receivables. It was **superseded by NFRS 9** from 16 July 2021 (1 Shrawan 2078 BS), but understanding NAS 39 is still important because:

1. Historical financial statements (before 2078 BS) were prepared under NAS 39
2. Exam questions still test the transition from NAS 39 to NFRS 9
3. The conceptual differences highlight why IFRS evolved

Under NAS 39, financial assets were classified into **four categories** based on management's intention:
- **Fair Value through Profit or Loss (FVTPL)** — Held for trading or designated. Measured at fair value with gains/losses in P&L.
- **Held to Maturity (HTM)** — Bonds/debentures the entity intends and is able to hold until maturity. Measured at amortised cost.
- **Loans and Receivables (L&R)** — Non-derivative assets with fixed/determinable payments not quoted in an active market. Measured at amortised cost.
- **Available for Sale (AFS)** — Everything else. Measured at fair value with gains/losses in OCI (recycled to P&L on disposal).

The biggest criticism of NAS 39 was its **incurred loss** impairment model — losses were only recognised when there was objective evidence of impairment (a "trigger event"). This meant bad debts were recognised too late, especially during financial crises.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 39 implemented a **mixed measurement model** driven by classification intent. Financial assets were classified into four categories at initial recognition, with limited reclassification permitted. This intent-based model was criticised for being complex, rule-based, and allowing earnings management through classification choices.

**Classification and measurement:**
- **FVTPL** — Held-for-trading (acquired for short-term profit) or designated at initial recognition (to eliminate accounting mismatch). Fair value gains/losses → P&L.
- **HTM** — Debt instruments with fixed maturity that the entity has positive intent and ability to hold to maturity. Amortised cost using effective interest rate. Tainting provision: selling more than insignificant HTM holdings reclassifies the entire portfolio out of HTM for two years.
- **L&R** — Non-derivative, fixed/determinable payments, not quoted in active market. Amortised cost. This was the default for bank loans, trade receivables, and intercompany loans.
- **AFS** — Residual category. Fair value with unrealised gains/losses in OCI; on disposal, the cumulative OCI is **recycled** (reclassified) to P&L. Interest income on AFS debt instruments recognised in P&L using effective interest rate.

**Impairment under NAS 39:**
The incurred loss model required objective evidence of impairment — a loss event that had already occurred. For individually significant assets, assessment was individual; for portfolios, collective assessment using historical loss rates. This backward-looking approach meant that expected future losses were not recognised, leading to the "too little, too late" criticism that ultimately drove the move to NFRS 9's expected credit loss (ECL) model.

In Nepal, NRB's transitional directives allowed BFIs to phase in from NAS 39 to NFRS 9 over two years (FY 2078/79 and 2079/80), recognising the significant increase in impairment provisions under ECL compared to the incurred loss model.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 39 was adopted in Nepal as part of the NFRS convergence effective 16 July 2018 (1 Shrawan 2075 BS), giving Nepal only a three-year window with NAS 39 before transitioning to NFRS 9 on 16 July 2021. This compressed timeline meant many entities — particularly Class A and B BFIs — were simultaneously learning NAS 39 while preparing for NFRS 9. Some entities effectively skipped full NAS 39 implementation and moved directly to NFRS 9.

**Hedge accounting under NAS 39:**
NAS 39's hedge accounting was rules-based with strict effectiveness testing requirements: prospective and retrospective effectiveness within the 80%–125% band. Three hedge types: fair value hedge (hedged item remeasured for hedged risk, offset in P&L), cash flow hedge (effective portion in OCI, ineffective in P&L), and net investment hedge. In practice, very few Nepali entities applied hedge accounting under NAS 39 — derivative markets are nascent, and NRB restricted most derivative instruments for BFIs to plain-vanilla forwards and swaps.

**Transition impact — NAS 39 to NFRS 9:**
The transition was seismic for Nepal's banking sector. Key impacts:
- **Classification:** NAS 39's four categories collapsed into NFRS 9's three (amortised cost, FVOCI, FVTPL). The business model test and SPPI test replaced intent-based classification. Many L&R and HTM instruments migrated to amortised cost; AFS equity instruments had an irrevocable FVOCI election; AFS debt instruments were reclassified based on business model.
- **Impairment:** The shift from incurred loss to ECL was the largest P&L/equity impact. NRB data showed aggregate additional provisions of approximately NPR 20–30 billion across the banking sector upon NFRS 9 adoption. Stage 1 (12-month ECL) applied to performing loans, Stage 2 (lifetime ECL) to those with significant increase in credit risk, Stage 3 (lifetime ECL on gross carrying amount) to credit-impaired.
- **Equity impact:** Transitional adjustments were recognised in opening retained earnings. NRB allowed phased recognition over two years for capital adequacy purposes, preventing a sudden capital shock.

The "tainting" provision in NAS 39 HTM was particularly problematic in Nepal: NRB's liquidity requirements sometimes forced BFIs to sell government securities classified as HTM, triggering reclassification of entire HTM portfolios. NFRS 9 resolved this by basing classification on business model assessment at portfolio level rather than intent at individual instrument level.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 39 applied to all financial instruments — assets and liabilities — except those specifically excluded (like interests in subsidiaries under NFRS 10, leases under NAS 17/NFRS 16, and employee benefits under NAS 19).

**Key terms:**
- **Financial instrument** — A contract that gives rise to a financial asset of one entity and a financial liability or equity instrument of another. Example: a bank loan is a financial asset for the bank and a financial liability for the borrower.
- **Fair value** — The price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction. NEPSE closing price for listed shares; valuation models for unquoted instruments.
- **Amortised cost** — The amount at initial recognition minus principal repayments, plus or minus cumulative amortisation of any premium/discount using the effective interest rate, minus impairment.
- **Effective interest rate (EIR)** — The rate that exactly discounts estimated future cash flows through the expected life of the instrument to its net carrying amount.
- **Impairment** — A reduction in value when there is objective evidence that the entity will not collect all amounts due.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

Scope exclusions from NAS 39 include: interests in subsidiaries, associates and JVs (NFRS 10/NAS 28); lease receivables and payables (NAS 17/NFRS 16); employer's assets and liabilities under employee benefit plans (NAS 19); insurance contracts (NFRS 4); financial guarantee contracts issued (unless designated at FVTPL); and contingent consideration in a business combination (NFRS 3).

Key measurement concepts:
- **Transaction costs** — Included in initial measurement of all financial assets/liabilities except FVTPL (where they are expensed immediately).
- **Day 1 gain/loss** — When fair value at initial recognition differs from transaction price and fair value is not evidenced by a quoted price or observable inputs, NAS 39 defers the day 1 gain/loss. This was particularly relevant for Nepal's banking sector with illiquid instruments.
- **Embedded derivatives** — NAS 39 required bifurcation of embedded derivatives from host contracts when not closely related and the hybrid was not at FVTPL. Common in Nepal: convertible debentures, structured deposits with equity-linked returns. NFRS 9 simplified this for financial asset hosts (no bifurcation — classify the whole instrument).
- **Derecognition** — NAS 39's derecognition rules (pass-through and continuing involvement tests) were retained unchanged in NFRS 9.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The embedded derivative bifurcation rules under NAS 39 created significant practical challenges in Nepal. NRB permitted structured products (e.g., dual-currency deposits, equity-linked notes) that contained embedded derivatives not closely related to the host contract. Bifurcation required fair value measurement of the embedded derivative — often requiring valuation models and market data not readily available in Nepal's thin markets.

NAS 39's scope exclusion for loan commitments was nuanced: loan commitments that could be settled net in cash, or that the entity had a past practice of selling the loans shortly after origination, were within scope. Nepal's banking sector — where loan portfolios are generally held to collect — rarely encountered this in-scope scenario, but consortium lending arrangements with lead bank sell-down features could trigger it.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

Under NAS 39, an entity recognised a financial asset or liability when it became a party to the contractual provisions of the instrument (<ParaRef std="IAS 39" para="14" />). For regular-way purchases/sales of financial assets, the entity could choose either trade date or settlement date accounting, applied consistently for each category.

Initial measurement was at fair value, which was normally the transaction price. For FVTPL instruments, transaction costs were expensed; for all other categories, transaction costs were included in initial measurement.

Derecognition of financial assets followed the complex flow chart: first assess whether the rights to cash flows have expired; if not, whether the entity has transferred the asset; if transferred, whether it has transferred substantially all risks and rewards; if neither transferred nor retained substantially all, whether it retains control. This framework was retained unchanged in NFRS 9.

## Measurement

**Subsequent measurement by category:**
- **FVTPL:** Fair value, changes in P&L. No impairment test needed (fair value already reflects credit risk).
- **HTM:** Amortised cost using EIR. Subject to impairment testing.
- **L&R:** Amortised cost using EIR. Subject to impairment testing. This was the primary category for bank loan portfolios in Nepal.
- **AFS:** Fair value, unrealised changes in OCI (AFS reserve in equity). On disposal, cumulative OCI recycled to P&L. AFS equity instruments without reliable fair value were permitted to remain at cost. Subject to impairment testing — when impaired, cumulative loss in OCI was reclassified to P&L, and for equity instruments, impairment was not reversed through P&L.

**Impairment triggers** (objective evidence): significant financial difficulty of borrower, breach of contract (default/delinquency), lender granting concession, probability of borrower entering bankruptcy, disappearance of active market, observable decrease in estimated future cash flows from a portfolio.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

NAS 39 itself was primarily a recognition and measurement standard. Disclosure requirements were in NAS 32 (Financial Instruments: Presentation — offsetting criteria, classification of equity vs liability) and NFRS 7 (Financial Instruments: Disclosures — risk disclosures, fair value hierarchy, sensitivity analysis).

Key presentation requirements under NAS 32 applicable alongside NAS 39: financial assets and liabilities were offset only when there was a legally enforceable right to set off and an intention to settle net. Compound instruments (e.g., convertible debentures) were split into liability and equity components.

Transition disclosures from NAS 39 to NFRS 9 required reconciliation of carrying amounts by NAS 39 category to NFRS 9 category, explanation of reclassifications, and quantification of impairment change from incurred loss to ECL model.
