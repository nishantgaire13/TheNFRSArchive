## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

When a company buys a large chunk of another company — say 25% or 35% — it doesn't fully own that company, but it has enough shares to influence key decisions like appointing directors or setting dividend policy. This kind of investment is called an "associate." Think of it like being a senior partner in a business: you don't run it alone, but your voice matters.

NAS 28 tells entities how to account for these investments using the "equity method." Instead of just recording the shares at what you paid, you update the value each year to reflect your share of the investee's profits or losses. If Nabil Bank owns 30% of an insurance company that earns NPR 10 crore, Nabil records NPR 3 crore as its share of profit — even if no cash dividend is received.

**Why does this matter?** Nepal's banking sector frequently holds significant stakes in insurance companies, finance companies, and microfinance institutions. Getting associate accounting right directly affects a bank's reported earnings and capital adequacy.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 28 prescribes the equity method for accounting for investments where the investor has significant influence (associates) or joint control (joint ventures). Significant influence is presumed at 20% or more of voting power, though this is rebuttable — an entity with 18% but board representation may still have significant influence.

Under the equity method, the investment is initially recognised at cost and subsequently adjusted for the investor's share of the investee's profit or loss, other comprehensive income, and distributions. This is fundamentally different from fair-value accounting under NFRS 9 — the equity method reflects a "one-line consolidation" approach that captures the investor's economic interest in the associate's net assets.

In Nepal, BFI cross-holdings are pervasive: commercial banks hold stakes in insurance companies, development banks, and microfinance institutions. These investments are material to the investor's financial statements and the equity method captures economic reality better than historical cost. NAS 28 is fully aligned with IAS 28 — no carve-outs exist.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 28 implements the equity method as defined in <ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="3" /> — a method of accounting whereby the investment is initially recognised at cost and adjusted thereafter for the post-acquisition change in the investor's share of the investee's net assets. The standard's significance-influence rebuttable presumption (<ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="5" />) at 20% voting power must be applied carefully in Nepal's concentrated ownership environment, where promoter groups may exercise significant influence through board seats, key management personnel, or inter-entity transactions despite holding less than 20%.

The equity method's interaction with impairment is architecturally important: <ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="40–43" /> requires the investor to apply NAS 36 impairment indicators to the entire carrying amount (including goodwill embedded in cost). Nepal's illiquid private equity market makes recoverable-amount estimation particularly challenging — there is no active market quotation for most associates, forcing reliance on value-in-use calculations with subjective cash flow projections.

Upstream and downstream transaction elimination (<ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="28" />) is often overlooked by Nepali entities. When an associate sells goods to the investor, unrealised profit in closing inventory must be eliminated to the extent of the investor's interest. BFI cross-holdings create complex inter-entity lending and fee arrangements that require careful elimination.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope & key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 28 covers investments where a company has **significant influence** — generally 20% to 50% of voting shares. Below 20% is usually just a financial investment (NFRS 9). Above 50% is a subsidiary (NFRS 10 — full consolidation).

**Key terms:**
- **Associate** — A company over which the investor has significant influence but not control. Like having a strong voice but not the final say.
- **Significant influence** — The power to participate in financial and operating policy decisions, but not control them.
- **Equity method** — Adjusting your investment's value each year by your share of the associate's profits or losses.
- **Joint venture** — An arrangement where two or more parties have joint control and rights to the net assets.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The standard applies to all entities that have significant influence or joint control over an investee, except venture capital organisations, mutual funds, and similar entities that elect fair value through profit or loss under NFRS 9 (<ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="18" />).

Key definitions:
- **Significant influence** — power to participate in financial and operating policy decisions, evidenced by board representation, policy-making participation, material inter-entity transactions, management interchange, or essential technical information provision.
- **Equity method** — initial recognition at cost, subsequent adjustment for the investor's share of post-acquisition changes in the investee's net assets.
- **Joint venture** — a joint arrangement under NFRS 11 where parties with joint control have rights to net assets rather than rights to individual assets and obligations for liabilities.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

Scope complexities in Nepal: (a) BFIs holding 15–19% stakes in other financial institutions with board seats — the 20% presumption is rebutted, and significant influence exists through board representation (<ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="6" />), (b) investments held by venture capital subsidiaries of banks — the exemption in <ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="18" /> allows FVTPL measurement but requires irrevocable election, (c) NRB's directive on inter-BFI shareholding limits (15% for commercial banks, 25% for development banks) constrains the ownership range but does not eliminate equity method applicability, (d) interaction with NFRS 10 — loss of control but retained significant influence triggers remeasurement of retained interest at fair value, then equity method application going forward (<ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="22" />).

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

An investment in an associate or joint venture is initially recognised at cost (<ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="10" />). Cost includes the purchase price plus any directly attributable transaction costs. From the acquisition date, the investor applies the equity method: the carrying amount is increased or decreased by the investor's share of the investee's profit or loss, recognised in the investor's profit or loss. Distributions (dividends) received reduce the carrying amount and are not recognised as income. If the investor's share of losses equals or exceeds the carrying amount, the investor discontinues recognising further losses unless it has incurred legal or constructive obligations or made payments on behalf of the investee (<ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="38" />).

## Measurement

**Equity method mechanics:** The investor's share of profit or loss is based on the investee's financial statements prepared using uniform accounting policies. If the associate uses different policies, adjustments are required (<ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="35" />). The investee's reporting date should not differ by more than three months from the investor's. In Nepal, mismatched fiscal years are uncommon since most entities follow the Shrawan-to-Ashad fiscal year, but entities with foreign associates may face reporting-date differences.

**Impairment:** After applying the equity method, the investor assesses whether there is objective evidence of impairment. The entire carrying amount (including embedded goodwill) is tested as a single asset under NAS 36. Impairment loss is recognised in profit or loss and is reversible if the recoverable amount subsequently increases (<ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="40–43" />).

## Presentation & disclosure

The investor presents its share of the associate's profit or loss as a single line item in profit or loss (after tax). The investment is classified as a non-current asset on the balance sheet. Key disclosures include the nature and extent of significant restrictions on the associate's ability to transfer funds (dividends, loan repayments), unrecognised share of losses, summarised financial information of each material associate, and the fair value of investments in associates for which published price quotations exist (<ParaRef std="IAS 28" para="36–37" />). In Nepal, SEBON requires listed entities to separately disclose investments in associates in quarterly filings, including the basis of significant-influence determination.
