## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Sometimes, an asset loses value. A factory machine might become outdated. A hotel building might lose value because tourist numbers dropped. When the value an asset can generate (through use or sale) falls below its book value, the asset is "impaired."

NAS 36 requires businesses to check for impairment and write down the asset's value when needed. Think of it as a reality check: "Is this asset still worth what our books say?"

**The basic rule:** If an asset's **carrying amount** (book value) is higher than its **recoverable amount** (what you can get from it), you must reduce the book value and record a loss.

**Recoverable amount** is the higher of:
- What you could sell it for (minus selling costs)
- The value of future income it will generate (discounted to today's value)

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 36 ensures that an entity's assets are not carried at more than their recoverable amount — the higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use. When an asset's carrying amount exceeds its recoverable amount, the asset is impaired and a loss must be recognised.

This standard is especially important in Nepal's current economic context. Hydropower projects facing construction delays, hotels with declining occupancy, manufacturing plants with excess capacity, and goodwill from acquisitions in the banking sector all present impairment risk. NAS 36 provides the framework for testing these assets and measuring any write-down.

NAS 36 is fully aligned with IAS 36.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 36 implements an impairment framework that intersects with virtually every asset-recognition standard in the NFRS suite. The recoverable amount concept — higher of FVLCD and VIU — creates an asymmetric measurement: assets are not written up above cost (except under revaluation models), but are written down to recoverable amount when indicators exist.

The CGU concept is the standard's most analytically demanding element. Cash-generating unit identification requires analysis of asset interdependencies and revenue independence — a non-trivial exercise for vertically integrated Nepali conglomerates where divisions share infrastructure, customers, and supply chains. Goodwill allocation to CGUs creates a "ceiling test" that often triggers impairment before individual asset-level indicators manifest.

The standard's discount rate requirements (pre-tax, risk-adjusted) create circular-reference issues: the discount rate should reflect risks not already adjusted in the cash flows, but distinguishing entity-specific risk from market risk requires capital-market data that Nepal's shallow equity market provides imperfectly.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope & key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 36 applies to most long-term assets like buildings, machinery, and goodwill. It does NOT apply to: inventory (NAS 2 handles that with NRV), financial assets (NFRS 9), or investment property measured at fair value (NAS 40).

**Key terms:**
- **Carrying amount** — The value of the asset on your books (cost minus depreciation so far).
- **Recoverable amount** — What you can actually get from the asset — either by selling it or using it.
- **Value in use** — The present value of all future cash the asset will generate.
- **Cash-generating unit (CGU)** — When you cannot test a single asset alone, you test the smallest group of assets that generates income independently.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The standard applies to all assets except inventories (NAS 2), financial assets (NFRS 9), investment property at fair value (NAS 40), and biological assets at fair value (NAS 41) (<ParaRef std="IAS 36" para="2" />).

Key terms:
- **Recoverable amount** — higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use.
- **Value in use** — present value of future cash flows from the asset or CGU.
- **Cash-generating unit (CGU)** — smallest group of assets generating independent cash inflows.
- **Impairment loss** — amount by which carrying amount exceeds recoverable amount.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

CGU identification is the critical analytical step. The standard defines CGUs by cash inflow independence (<ParaRef std="IAS 36" para="66" />), but in practice, Nepali entities must consider: transfer pricing arrangements between divisions (arm's-length pricing suggests independence), shared assets (allocated to CGUs on a reasonable and consistent basis), and corporate assets (cannot generate independent cash flows — allocated down to CGUs for testing).

Goodwill acquired in business combinations is allocated to CGUs expected to benefit from the synergies — not necessarily the CGUs to which the acquired assets are assigned. This allocation decision drives the level at which impairment testing occurs and can significantly affect whether impairment is detected.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

At each reporting date, assess indicators of impairment. Mandatory annual testing (regardless of indicators) for: goodwill, indefinite-life intangibles, and intangibles not yet available for use.

Impairment loss recognised immediately in P&L — unless the asset is revalued, in which case loss first reduces revaluation surplus (<ParaRef std="IAS 36" para="60" />).

## Measurement

**Value in use:** estimate future cash flows (typically 5-year projections plus terminal value), discount at pre-tax rate reflecting time value and asset-specific risks (<ParaRef std="IAS 36" para="30" />).

**CGU impairment allocation:** loss allocated first to goodwill, then pro-rata to other assets (<ParaRef std="IAS 36" para="104" />).

**Reversal:** permitted for all assets except goodwill. Cannot exceed carrying amount that would have existed without impairment (<ParaRef std="IAS 36" para="124" />).

## Presentation & disclosure

Disclose events leading to impairment, amount, nature of asset/CGU, recoverable amount basis, and discount rate used (<ParaRef std="IAS 36" para="126" />).
