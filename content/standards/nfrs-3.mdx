## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

When one company buys another company, or two companies merge into one, this is called a business combination. Think of it like two rivers joining together — the result is a single, larger river. In Nepal, the most visible example has been the wave of bank mergers directed by Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB). Nepal went from over 200 banks and financial institutions (BFIs) down to around 70 through forced mergers, as NRB required all BFIs to meet a minimum paid-up capital of NPR 8 arba (billion).

NFRS 3 tells companies how to account for these combinations. The basic rule is simple: one company is always identified as the "acquirer" — the one doing the buying. The acquirer records everything it gets (assets) and everything it owes (liabilities) at their fair values on the acquisition date. If the purchase price is higher than the fair value of net assets acquired, the difference is recorded as **goodwill** — an intangible asset representing things like brand value, customer relationships, and synergies.

For example, when Global IME Bank merged with Janata Bank Nepal, NFRS 3 required one entity to be identified as the acquirer. The acquirer then had to measure all of the acquired bank's assets (loans, buildings, investments) and liabilities (deposits, borrowings) at fair value, not just at whatever book values they had been carrying.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 3 prescribes the **acquisition method** as the sole method for accounting for business combinations — pooling of interests is not permitted. The acquisition method involves four steps: identifying the acquirer, determining the acquisition date, recognising and measuring the identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed at fair value, and recognising and measuring goodwill or a gain from a bargain purchase.

The standard requires identification of the acquirer — the entity that obtains control of the acquiree as defined in NFRS 10. In NRB-directed BFI mergers, identifying the acquirer can be complex because the merger may be regulatory-driven rather than market-driven. Factors include which entity issues new shares, which entity's management dominates the combined entity, and which entity pays a premium. In many Nepal BFI mergers, the larger institution is typically the acquirer even when the transaction is presented as a "merger of equals."

Purchase price allocation (PPA) is the most technically demanding step. The acquirer must identify and fair-value all identifiable intangible assets separately from goodwill — including customer deposit relationships, core deposit intangibles, loan portfolios, and brand names. In Nepal, the limited availability of qualified valuation professionals and comparable market data makes PPA a significant practical challenge for BFI mergers.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NFRS 3 operationalises the acquisition method through a hierarchy of measurement principles. The consideration transferred is measured at fair value (<ParaRef std="IFRS 3" para="37" />), which includes cash, other assets, liabilities incurred, and equity instruments issued by the acquirer. Contingent consideration — particularly common in Nepal BFI mergers where earn-out clauses or retention bonuses are negotiated — is measured at acquisition-date fair value and classified as either equity or a liability (<ParaRef std="IFRS 3" para="39-40" />). Liability-classified contingent consideration is remeasured at each reporting date with changes in profit or loss.

The recognition principle (<ParaRef std="IFRS 3" para="10-13" />) requires separate recognition of identifiable assets and liabilities that meet the Conceptual Framework definitions, even if not previously recognised by the acquiree. This is particularly relevant for BFIs where internally generated intangible assets (core deposit intangibles, customer relationships, developed technology platforms) were never on the acquiree's balance sheet. The recognition exception for contingent liabilities (<ParaRef std="IFRS 3" para="22-23" />) — recognised at acquisition-date fair value even if not probable — often surprises Nepali practitioners accustomed to NAS 37's probability threshold.

Nepal's BFI consolidation wave has exposed several challenges: (a) goodwill arising from NRB-directed mergers where purchase prices may not reflect arm's length negotiations; (b) bargain purchases generating gains in profit or loss when the acquired BFI's net assets exceed consideration transferred — frequently occurring when a struggling finance company is merged into a commercial bank; and (c) subsequent goodwill impairment testing under NAS 36 for BFIs where cash-generating unit identification is complicated by post-merger branch integration.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NFRS 3 applies whenever one entity acquires control of another entity — in other words, whenever a **business combination** occurs. It does **not** apply to joint ventures (covered by NFRS 11), combinations of entities under common control (e.g., restructuring within a group), or acquisitions of individual assets that do not constitute a business.

**Key terms:**
- **Business combination** — A transaction or event in which an acquirer obtains control of one or more businesses.
- **Acquirer** — The entity that obtains control of the other entity.
- **Goodwill** — The excess of the purchase price over the fair value of identifiable net assets. It represents future economic benefits that cannot be individually identified.
- **Bargain purchase** — When the fair value of net assets acquired exceeds the purchase price, resulting in a gain.
- **Contingent consideration** — An additional payment the acquirer may have to make depending on future events (e.g., profit targets being met).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 3 applies to transactions or events that meet the definition of a business combination (<ParaRef std="IFRS 3" para="3" />). A critical threshold question is whether the acquired set of activities and assets constitutes a "business" — if not, the transaction is an asset acquisition accounted for differently (no goodwill, costs allocated to identifiable assets proportionally). The optional concentration test (<ParaRef std="IFRS 3" para="B7A-B7B" />) allows entities to conclude an acquisition is not a business if substantially all fair value is concentrated in a single asset or group of similar assets.

Key technical definitions:
- **Control** — Power over the investee, exposure or rights to variable returns, and ability to use power to affect returns (per NFRS 10).
- **Identifiable intangible assets** — Must be separable (capable of being sold, transferred, or licensed separately) or arise from contractual or legal rights (<ParaRef std="IFRS 3" para="B31-B33" />). Examples: banking licenses, insurance licenses, customer lists.
- **Measurement period** — Up to one year from the acquisition date during which the acquirer may adjust provisional amounts recognised (<ParaRef std="IFRS 3" para="45" />). Commonly used in Nepal BFI mergers where loan portfolio valuations require extended due diligence.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope exclusion for combinations of entities under common control is significant in Nepal where conglomerate groups restructure holdings frequently. No IFRS specifically addresses common control transactions, creating a policy choice: entities may apply the acquisition method (NFRS 3) or a book-value (predecessor accounting) method. In practice, most Nepali groups use book values for internal restructurings, but the absence of authoritative guidance leads to inconsistent treatment.

The business-versus-asset-acquisition distinction has become more critical following the concentration test introduction. In Nepal, this is particularly relevant for: (a) acquisition of a single hotel property with staff and booking systems — is it a business or an asset?; (b) purchase of a hydropower project company whose sole asset is a single power purchase agreement — the concentration test may indicate asset acquisition; and (c) acquisition of a finance company primarily for its banking license — the license may be the only significant asset, yet the company has employees, processes, and depositors constituting a business.

Interaction with NFRS 10 is essential: NFRS 3 accounts for the combination transaction, while NFRS 10 governs ongoing consolidation. Step acquisitions — where an entity moves from an associate (NAS 28) or joint venture (NFRS 11) to a subsidiary — require remeasurement of the previously held interest at acquisition-date fair value with the gain or loss in profit or loss (<ParaRef std="IFRS 3" para="42" />).

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

NFRS 3 requires the acquirer to recognise, separately from goodwill, the identifiable assets acquired, liabilities assumed, and any non-controlling interest in the acquiree at the acquisition date. The recognition principle requires that items meet the definition of assets and liabilities in the Conceptual Framework at the acquisition date — even if they were not previously recognised by the acquiree. Contingent liabilities are recognised at fair value if present obligations arising from past events exist, regardless of probability.

In Nepal's BFI merger context, the most significant recognised items include: loan portfolios (remeasured at fair value reflecting expected credit losses), property and equipment (often revalued, particularly prime Kathmandu real estate), core deposit intangibles (the value of stable, low-cost deposit relationships), and any regulatory provisions or contingent liabilities from NRB directives or pending litigation. The measurement period (up to 12 months) is frequently utilised because post-merger loan portfolio reviews often reveal credit quality issues not apparent at the acquisition date.

## Measurement

Identifiable assets and liabilities are measured at acquisition-date fair value, with limited exceptions including deferred tax (measured per NAS 12 without discounting), employee benefits (measured per NAS 19), and indemnification assets (measured on the same basis as the indemnified item). Goodwill is measured as the excess of consideration transferred plus any non-controlling interest plus the fair value of previously held equity interest, over the net identifiable assets acquired.

For Nepal BFI mergers, the most challenging measurement exercises involve: loan portfolio fair values (requiring probability-weighted cash flow models for large heterogeneous portfolios, factoring in NRB provisioning norms and sector-specific default rates), core deposit intangible valuation (requiring assumptions about deposit attrition rates and alternative cost of funding), and property valuations (where Kathmandu real estate prices can differ dramatically between government-assessed values, book values, and market transactions). The limited market for valuation services in Nepal means that many entities rely on a small pool of chartered accountants and merchant bankers for these exercises.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

NFRS 3 requires extensive disclosures to help users evaluate the nature and financial effect of business combinations. The acquirer must disclose: the name and description of the acquiree, the acquisition date, the percentage of voting equity acquired, the primary reasons for the combination and how control was obtained, the acquisition-date fair value of total consideration (broken down by cash, equity, and contingent consideration), and the amounts recognised for each major class of assets and liabilities.

For goodwill, the entity must disclose the amount and the factors contributing to it (expected synergies, intangible assets not qualifying for separate recognition). If a bargain purchase gain arises, the entity must describe the reasons. Measurement period adjustments must be disclosed if provisional amounts are revised. In Nepal, additional disclosures may be required by NRB for BFI mergers (including regulatory capital impact, branch integration plans, and staff rationalisation provisions) and by SEBON for listed entities (including the effect on earnings per share and share exchange ratios communicated to shareholders).
