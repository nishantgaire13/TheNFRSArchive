## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Sometimes a company decides to sell a major asset — perhaps a factory, a hotel, or an entire division of the business. When management commits to selling and actively looks for a buyer, the asset is no longer being "used" in the normal business — it is being "held for sale." NFRS 5 establishes special rules for how to account for these assets once the decision to sell has been made.

Think of it like putting a "For Sale" sign on a house. Once you decide to sell your house and list it with a broker, you stop renovating it (no more depreciation) and you need to check whether the selling price will cover your investment. Similarly, NFRS 5 says that once an asset is classified as held for sale, the company stops depreciating it and measures it at the lower of its book value or its fair value minus the costs to sell.

In Nepal, common examples include BFIs closing branches in remote areas and selling the branch premises, hotel companies disposing of properties during downturns, manufacturing companies shutting down unprofitable production lines, and conglomerates divesting entire subsidiaries. When Himalayan Bank might decide to sell its old headquarters building in New Road because it has moved to a new location, NFRS 5 governs how that building appears in the financial statements from the moment the sale decision is made.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 5 specifies the accounting for non-current assets (or disposal groups) that an entity plans to recover principally through sale rather than continuing use. The standard introduces a dual framework: classification criteria for "held for sale" and "held for distribution," and separate presentation requirements for "discontinued operations."

For classification as held for sale, the asset must be available for immediate sale in its present condition (subject only to customary terms) and the sale must be **highly probable** — meaning management is committed to a plan to sell, an active programme to locate a buyer has been initiated, the asset is being actively marketed at a reasonable price, the sale is expected to be completed within one year, and it is unlikely the plan will be significantly changed or withdrawn.

Once classified, measurement shifts to the lower of carrying amount and fair value less costs to sell. Depreciation ceases. If fair value less costs to sell is lower than carrying amount, an impairment loss is recognised. In Nepal, practical issues include determining "fair value" for assets in illiquid markets (e.g., factory land in industrial estates like Bhaktapur or Hetauda where comparable transactions are scarce) and meeting the one-year sale completion criterion in a market where property transactions can take significantly longer due to regulatory approvals and buyer financing constraints.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NFRS 5 creates a measurement and presentation regime that overrides the normal requirements of other standards once classification criteria are met (<ParaRef std="IFRS 5" para="5" />). The "highly probable" threshold (<ParaRef std="IFRS 5" para="7-8" />) is higher than "more likely than not" and requires demonstrable management commitment, active marketing, and reasonable pricing — a board resolution alone is insufficient. The one-year expected completion period (<ParaRef std="IFRS 5" para="8" />) has limited exceptions for delays caused by events beyond the entity's control (<ParaRef std="IFRS 5" para="9" />).

Measurement interactions are nuanced: the impairment testing sequence matters. Before initial classification as held for sale, the entity must measure the asset under its applicable standard (e.g., NAS 16 revaluation, NAS 36 impairment) and then apply the NFRS 5 measurement of lower of adjusted carrying amount and fair value less costs to sell (<ParaRef std="IFRS 5" para="18" />). Deferred tax on temporary differences related to held-for-sale assets continues to be recognised under NAS 12.

Nepal-specific challenges include: (a) NRB-directed disposal of non-banking assets held by BFIs within prescribed timeframes — the NRB timeline may not align with NFRS 5's one-year criterion; (b) forced disposal of foreclosed collateral assets where the BFI is required to sell within a regulatory window but market conditions make the sale "highly probable" determination uncertain; (c) discontinued operations presentation for BFI subsidiaries being wound down as part of NRB's consolidation directives — requiring separate presentation of post-tax profit or loss and disposal gains/losses on the face of the statement of profit or loss (<ParaRef std="IFRS 5" para="33" />).

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NFRS 5 applies to **all non-current assets** and **disposal groups** that a company plans to sell. A disposal group is a collection of assets (and possibly related liabilities) that the company intends to sell together in a single transaction — for example, an entire hotel property including the building, furniture, and associated bank loans.

**Key terms:**
- **Held for sale** — An asset the company plans to sell rather than continue using, where the sale is expected within one year.
- **Disposal group** — A group of assets and liabilities to be disposed of together.
- **Discontinued operation** — A component of the entity (a separate business segment or geographical area) that has been disposed of or is classified as held for sale.
- **Fair value less costs to sell** — The price you would receive from selling the asset, minus broker fees, legal costs, and other selling expenses.
- **Highly probable** — More certain than "more likely than not" — the sale is almost definitely going to happen.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 5 applies to all recognised non-current assets and disposal groups except those specifically excluded: deferred tax assets (NAS 12), assets arising from employee benefits (NAS 19), financial assets within NFRS 9, non-current assets measured at fair value less costs to sell under NAS 41 (agriculture), and investment property measured at fair value under NAS 40 (<ParaRef std="IFRS 5" para="5" />). These exclusions exist because those standards already have their own fair value or measurement frameworks.

Key technical definitions:
- **Discontinued operation** — A component that either has been disposed of or is classified as held for sale, and represents a separate major line of business or geographical area of operations, is part of a single coordinated plan to dispose of a separate major line of business, or is a subsidiary acquired exclusively with a view to resale (<ParaRef std="IFRS 5" para="32" />).
- **Component of an entity** — Operations and cash flows that can be clearly distinguished, operationally and for financial reporting purposes, from the rest of the entity. Must have been a cash-generating unit or a group of cash-generating units while being held for use.
- **Costs to sell** — Incremental costs directly attributable to the disposal, excluding finance costs and income tax expense.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope exclusions create measurement asymmetries that require careful navigation. A disposal group may contain assets within NFRS 5's scope (PPE, intangibles) and assets excluded from its measurement provisions (financial instruments at fair value, deferred tax). When measuring the disposal group, excluded assets are measured under their respective standards first, and then NFRS 5's lower-of test applies only to the remaining assets in the group (<ParaRef std="IFRS 5" para="5" />). The impairment allocation within a disposal group follows the NAS 36 hierarchy — first to goodwill, then pro-rata to other assets within scope.

The distinction between a discontinued operation and a mere held-for-sale classification is critical for presentation. A single branch closure by a BFI is unlikely to constitute a discontinued operation (not a separate major line of business), but the disposal of an entire insurance subsidiary by a banking group could qualify. The "separate major line of business" criterion is judged relative to the entity, not the industry — a hotel chain selling one of twenty properties is not discontinuing a major line, but a conglomerate selling its only hotel business may be.

Interactions with NAS 36 are important: once classified as held for sale, NAS 36 impairment testing (value in use) no longer applies — the asset is measured only at fair value less costs to sell, not the higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use. This can result in larger impairment charges for assets whose value in use exceeded fair value less costs to sell prior to reclassification.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

NFRS 5 does not create new recognition criteria — assets and liabilities classified as held for sale were already recognised under other standards. Rather, NFRS 5 changes the classification and measurement of these items upon meeting the held-for-sale criteria. The entity reclassifies the non-current asset (or disposal group) to current assets on the statement of financial position and ceases depreciation or amortisation from the date of classification.

In practice, recognition of the held-for-sale classification in Nepal is often delayed because entities are reluctant to signal disposal intentions to the market — particularly for NEPSE-listed companies where the announcement could affect share price. However, NFRS 5 requires classification at the point the criteria are met, not when the entity chooses to announce. For BFIs, NRB directives on disposal of non-banking assets or foreclosed collateral create mandatory sale requirements that may trigger earlier classification than management would otherwise choose.

## Measurement

Upon classification as held for sale, a non-current asset (or disposal group) is measured at the **lower of its carrying amount and fair value less costs to sell**. Immediately before classification, the entity must measure the asset under its normal applicable standard (e.g., depreciate under NAS 16, test for impairment under NAS 36). If fair value less costs to sell is below the carrying amount, an impairment loss is recognised. Subsequent increases in fair value less costs to sell can reverse previous impairment losses, but only up to the cumulative impairment loss recognised under NFRS 5 and NAS 36.

In Nepal, determining fair value less costs to sell is challenging for several asset types: industrial land (government minimum rates vs. actual market rates), hotel properties (few comparable transactions, earnings-based valuations sensitive to tourism cycles), and BFI branch premises (restricted by NRB norms on property ownership). Costs to sell in Nepal often include significant stamp duties (property registration fees), capital gains tax considerations, and broker commissions that can collectively represent 5-8% of the transaction value.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

NFRS 5 requires held-for-sale assets and disposal groups to be presented separately from other assets and liabilities on the face of the statement of financial position. Assets and liabilities of a disposal group must be presented separately — they cannot be offset against each other. Comparative periods are not reclassified for held-for-sale assets (unlike discontinued operations).

For discontinued operations, the entity must present on the face of the statement of profit or loss (or in the notes): the post-tax profit or loss of the discontinued operation and the post-tax gain or loss on the measurement to fair value less costs to sell or on disposal. Cash flows from discontinued operations must be separately disclosed (operating, investing, financing). In Nepal, the most common discontinued operation disclosures arise from BFI group restructurings where subsidiaries are merged or wound down under NRB directives, and from conglomerates divesting non-core business segments. SEBON additionally requires immediate disclosure of any decision to discontinue a significant operation by listed entities.
