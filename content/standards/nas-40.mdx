## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

When a company buys land or a building to rent out or to hold because its value is going up, that property is called an **investment property**. It is different from a building the company uses for its own offices (that is PPE under NAS 16) or property held for sale in the ordinary course of business (that is inventory under NAS 2).

NAS 40 sets out how to account for investment properties. Think of a bank that owns a building in Kathmandu and rents it out to other businesses. That building is not used in the bank's banking operations — it is an investment generating rental income. NAS 40 applies.

The standard gives entities a choice: measure investment property at **cost** (like normal PPE with depreciation) or at **fair value** (revaluing to market price each year, with changes going to profit or loss). In Nepal, most companies choose the cost model because reliable fair values are difficult to obtain — even though Kathmandu Valley real estate prices have increased dramatically over the past 15 years.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 40 addresses the recognition, measurement, and disclosure of investment property — property (land or building, or part thereof) held by the owner or lessee (as a right-of-use asset) to earn rental income, for capital appreciation, or both. The standard distinguishes investment property from owner-occupied property (NAS 16) and property held for sale in the ordinary course of business (NAS 2).

The measurement choice between the **fair value model** and the **cost model** is a critical policy decision applied to the entity's entire investment property portfolio. Under the fair value model, gains and losses from changes in fair value are recognised in profit or loss — creating potentially significant volatility. Under the cost model, investment property is depreciated and tested for impairment like PPE.

In Nepal, the vast majority of entities use the cost model. This is partly practical — Nepal lacks a mature property valuation industry with RICS-qualified valuers, and transaction data for comparable properties is often opaque. It is also partly strategic — entities prefer to avoid the P&L volatility that fair value measurement would introduce, particularly given the dramatic appreciation of Kathmandu Valley real estate.

NAS 40 is fully aligned with IAS 40.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 40's interaction with NFRS 16 (Leases) expanded its scope: a right-of-use asset meeting the investment property definition can be accounted for under NAS 40 using the fair value model (<ParaRef std="IAS 40" para="6" />). This creates an exception to NFRS 16's general measurement requirements — a lessee can elect fair value through profit or loss for investment property ROU assets even though other ROU assets follow the cost model.

The classification boundary between NAS 40 and NAS 16 requires judgment where property has dual use. If a portion is held for rental and another portion is owner-occupied, the portions are accounted for separately only if they could be sold or leased under a finance lease separately (<ParaRef std="IAS 40" para="10" />). In Nepal, mixed-use commercial buildings in Kathmandu (ground floor retail rented out, upper floors owner-occupied) frequently raise this classification question.

BFIs with foreclosed properties present a unique challenge. When a bank forecloses on a borrower's property, the initial classification depends on management intent: held for sale (NAS 2/NFRS 5), investment property (NAS 40), or owner-occupied (NAS 16). NRB directives typically require BFIs to dispose of foreclosed property within specified periods, which may affect the "held for capital appreciation" criterion. The fair value measurement hierarchy under NFRS 13 applies when the fair value model is elected — and Level 1 inputs for property are essentially non-existent in Nepal.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 40 covers land and buildings (or parts of buildings) that a company holds to earn rent or because the property value is rising. It does NOT cover: buildings used by the company for its own business (NAS 16), property being built or developed for future sale (NAS 2), or property under construction for others.

**Key terms:**
- **Investment property** — Land or a building held to earn rental income or for capital appreciation, not for use in business operations or for sale.
- **Fair value model** — Measuring the property at its current market value each year, with gains or losses going directly to profit or loss.
- **Cost model** — Measuring the property at original cost minus depreciation and impairment — the same approach used for PPE.
- **Transfer** — When a property changes use (e.g., company stops using its own building and rents it out), it moves between NAS 16 and NAS 40.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

Investment property includes: land held for long-term capital appreciation, land held for undetermined future use, a building owned and leased under operating leases, a vacant building held to be leased, and property being constructed for future use as investment property (<ParaRef std="IAS 40" para="8" />).

Exclusions: property held for sale in ordinary course of business or being constructed for such sale (NAS 2), owner-occupied property (NAS 16), property leased to another entity under a finance lease, and property under construction or development on behalf of third parties (NAS 11/NFRS 15).

- **Owner-occupied property** — used in production, supply of goods/services, or administration. The key test: does the property serve the entity's operations or generate independent rental/appreciation returns?
- **Ancillary services** — if services provided are significant (e.g., hotel services), the property is owner-occupied under NAS 16, not investment property. A building where the owner provides security and maintenance only? Investment property. A serviced office complex with reception, IT, catering? Likely owner-occupied.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The "significance of ancillary services" test (<ParaRef std="IAS 40" para="12-13" />) creates classification challenges for Nepal's hospitality-adjacent real estate. Serviced apartments in Kathmandu, where landlords provide cleaning, security, and sometimes meals, sit in a grey zone — the judgement turns on whether services are a "significant" component. Hotels are definitively owner-occupied; bare rental is definitively investment property; serviced apartments require entity-specific analysis.

Property held by a parent and occupied by a subsidiary is investment property in the parent's separate financial statements but owner-occupied in the consolidated statements (<ParaRef std="IAS 40" para="15" />). This dual classification is common in Nepali group structures where holding companies own properties used by operating subsidiaries.

Land banking — where real estate companies acquire large tracts of land in Kathmandu Valley outskirts anticipating future urbanisation — raises classification questions. If the entity is a property developer, such land may be inventory (NAS 2). If it is held purely for capital appreciation with no intent to develop and sell in the ordinary course, it qualifies as investment property (NAS 40). Management intent and the entity's principal activities determine the classification.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

Investment property is recognised as an asset when it is probable that future economic benefits will flow to the entity and the cost can be measured reliably (<ParaRef std="IAS 40" para="16" />). This is the same general recognition criterion as NAS 16. Initial measurement is at cost, including transaction costs such as legal fees, property transfer taxes, and registration charges.

Subsequent expenditure is capitalised only if it enhances the property's ability to generate future economic benefits beyond its original assessed standard. Day-to-day servicing costs (repairs, maintenance) are expensed as incurred. In Nepal, property registration costs paid to the Land Revenue Office and stamp duties form part of the initial cost.

## Measurement

After initial recognition, an entity chooses either the fair value model or the cost model and applies it consistently to all investment property (<ParaRef std="IAS 40" para="30" />). Under the **fair value model**, investment property is remeasured at each reporting date and gains/losses are recognised in profit or loss — not OCI. Under the **cost model**, investment property follows NAS 16 measurement (cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment), but fair value must still be disclosed in notes.

Transfers between investment property, owner-occupied property, and inventory are made only when there is a change in use evidenced by specific events (<ParaRef std="IAS 40" para="57" />). When transferring from fair value investment property to owner-occupied (NAS 16), the fair value at the date of change becomes the deemed cost for subsequent accounting.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

For the fair value model, entities disclose: methods and significant assumptions for determining fair value, the extent to which fair value is based on valuation by a qualified independent valuer, amounts recognised in profit or loss from rental income and direct operating expenses, and any restrictions on realisability or remittance of income (<ParaRef std="IAS 40" para="75" />).

For the cost model, entities disclose: depreciation methods and useful lives, gross carrying amount and accumulated depreciation/impairment, a reconciliation of carrying amounts, and the fair value of investment property. If fair value cannot be determined reliably, additional disclosures are required including a description of the property and explanation of why fair value cannot be measured.
