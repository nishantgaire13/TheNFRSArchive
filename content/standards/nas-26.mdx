## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 19 tells **employers** how to account for employee benefits. NAS 26 is different — it tells the **retirement benefit plan itself** how to prepare its own financial statements.

Think of Nepal's Employees' Provident Fund (EPF). EPF collects contributions from employers and employees, invests that money, and eventually pays out retirement benefits. EPF needs its own set of financial statements to show: how much money it has, how it invested that money, and whether it has enough to pay everyone who is owed benefits. That is what NAS 26 covers.

In Nepal, several major retirement benefit plans exist: the **Employees' Provident Fund (EPF)** established under the EPF Act 2019 BS, the **Citizen Investment Trust (CIT)** operating retirement savings schemes, and the **Social Security Fund (SSF)** under the Social Security Act 2074 BS. Private sector gratuity funds set up as separate trusts also fall under NAS 26 when they prepare their own financial statements.

The key idea: plan assets must be shown at **fair value**, and for defined benefit plans, the financial statements must also show the **actuarial present value of promised retirement benefits** — essentially, how much the plan would need today to pay all future benefits already earned by members.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 26 prescribes accounting and reporting standards for retirement benefit plans as reporting entities in their own right, distinct from NAS 19 which addresses the employer's accounting. The standard applies to plans that prepare separate financial statements — whether defined contribution or defined benefit.

For **defined contribution plans**, NAS 26 requires disclosure of: a statement of net assets available for benefits, a statement of changes in net assets, a description of the funding policy, and a summary of significant accounting policies (<ParaRef std="IAS 26" para="13–16" />). The key information is the fair value of plan assets and details of contributions received and benefits paid.

For **defined benefit plans**, NAS 26 requires either: (a) a statement showing the net assets available for benefits, the actuarial present value of promised retirement benefits (split between vested and non-vested), and the resulting surplus or deficit; or (b) a statement of net assets with a reference to an accompanying actuarial valuation report (<ParaRef std="IAS 26" para="17–21" />).

Nepal's EPF is a defined contribution plan from the employer's perspective (employer contributes 10%, obligation ends). But from EPF's own perspective as a reporting entity, EPF guarantees a minimum return to members — creating an element of defined benefit risk within EPF's own financial statements. CIT retirement schemes similarly blend DC contributions with guaranteed-return features.

Plan assets must be reported at **fair value** (<ParaRef std="IAS 26" para="32" />). For EPF, this means its investments in government bonds, corporate deposits, shares, and real estate must all be stated at fair value rather than cost — a significant difference from how EPF historically reported under the Fund's own regulations.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 26 addresses a niche but consequential reporting domain: the financial statements of the retirement benefit plan entity itself. While NAS 19 looks at the plan from the employer's viewpoint (measuring DBO and plan assets to determine net liability), NAS 26 looks from the plan's viewpoint — whether the plan has sufficient assets to meet its obligations.

The critical distinction for defined benefit plans is the **actuarial present value of promised retirement benefits (APVPRB)** (<ParaRef std="IAS 26" para="17" />). This is conceptually similar to the DBO under NAS 19 but calculated from the plan's perspective. NAS 26 permits two presentation approaches: (a) include the APVPRB on the face of the plan's statement of net assets, showing a surplus or deficit; or (b) present net assets available for benefits with a cross-reference to a separate actuarial report. Most plans globally — and in Nepal — adopt approach (b) due to the complexity and cost of actuarial valuations.

Nepal's retirement benefit landscape presents unique challenges. The **EPF** operates under the EPF Act 2019 BS (with amendments) and is governed by a tripartite board. EPF's investment portfolio includes government securities, bank fixed deposits, corporate debentures, and equity shares — but historical reporting used amortised cost for most instruments. Fair value measurement under NAS 26 / NFRS 13 reveals unrealised gains and losses previously hidden. EPF's guaranteed return promise (historically 7–9%) creates a hybrid DC/DB character: employers treat it as DC (contributions are 10% of basic, no further obligation), but EPF itself bears the investment risk of delivering promised returns.

The **Social Security Fund (SSF)**, established under the Social Security Act 2074 (2017), is newer and still building its reporting framework. SSF covers retirement pension, medical, accident, and maternity benefits. The retirement portion — 11% employer + 11% employee of basic salary — is structured as a defined benefit pension (guaranteed monthly pension after 15 years of contribution). SSF's plan financial statements must therefore show the actuarial present value of these promised pensions — a massive exercise given the young age of the scheme and the limited actuarial data available in Nepal.

Discount rate selection for APVPRB follows the same challenge as NAS 19: Nepal lacks a deep corporate bond market, so government bond yields (NRB development bonds, 6–9% range) serve as the reference. For SSF with pension obligations stretching 30–40 years, extrapolation beyond available bond tenors introduces significant estimation uncertainty.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 26 applies to the **financial statements of retirement benefit plans** — the plan is the reporting entity, not the employer.

**Key terms:**
- **Retirement benefit plan** — An arrangement where an entity provides benefits to employees on retirement (pension, lump sum, or both).
- **Defined contribution plan** — The employer and/or employee contribute fixed amounts. The final benefit depends on how well the investments perform. Example: Nepal's EPF contributions of 10% + 10%.
- **Defined benefit plan** — The plan promises a specific benefit amount. Example: SSF pension promising a monthly pension based on years of contribution and average salary.
- **Net assets available for benefits** — Total plan assets (at fair value) minus any liabilities other than the actuarial present value of promised benefits.
- **Actuarial present value of promised retirement benefits** — The present value (in today's money) of all future benefit payments the plan has promised, based on service already completed.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 26 applies to plans that are separate reporting entities preparing their own general purpose financial statements. It does not apply to employer financial statements (NAS 19 covers that) or to general employee welfare programmes that are not retirement-focused.

Key technical definitions:
- **Vested benefits** — Benefits that are not conditional on continued employment. Under Nepal's Labour Act 2074, gratuity vests after one year of continuous service — once vested, the employee is entitled regardless of future service.
- **Non-vested benefits** — Benefits conditional on future service. A pension requiring 15 years of contributions (as in SSF) has non-vested benefits until the member reaches 15 years.
- **Plan assets** — All assets held by the plan, reported at fair value (<ParaRef std="IAS 26" para="32" />). Includes government securities, fixed deposits, equities, real estate investments, and any qualifying insurance policies.
- **Funding policy** — The approach used to determine contributions necessary to meet the plan's benefit obligations. For statutory plans like EPF, funding is set by law (10% + 10%). For private gratuity trusts, actuarial funding recommendations guide employer contributions.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope interaction between NAS 26 and NAS 19 is critical. NAS 19 views the plan from the sponsoring employer's side (liability on employer's balance sheet). NAS 26 views from the plan's side (whether plan assets cover promised benefits). The same plan appears in two sets of financial statements with different measurement objectives.

Multi-employer plans present complexity: when multiple employers contribute to a single plan (e.g., EPF covering all formal sector employees), each employer applies NAS 19 from its own perspective (recognising only its share of contributions as DC expense), while EPF as the plan entity applies NAS 26 for its aggregate reporting. The lack of segregation of assets by employer within EPF means individual employer-level surplus/deficit analysis is not feasible — reinforcing the DC treatment at the employer level.

The distinction between vested and non-vested benefits in APVPRB is relevant for SSF's pension scheme: benefits vest after 15 years of contribution. For a member with 8 years of contributions, only a proportional accrued benefit (or nil, depending on plan terms for early departure) would be included in vested benefits. The APVPRB calculation must apply mortality tables, disability rates, and withdrawal assumptions specific to Nepal's demographics — data that is still being developed for SSF.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

For defined contribution plans, the plan's financial statements are relatively straightforward: recognise contributions received from employers and employees as additions to net assets, recognise investment income (interest, dividends, fair value changes) when earned, and recognise benefit payments and administrative expenses when incurred.

For defined benefit plans, the plan must additionally determine the APVPRB through actuarial valuation. This valuation may be performed using the projected unit credit method (consistent with NAS 19) or an accrued benefit method. The choice of actuarial method affects the magnitude of the APVPRB and must be disclosed.

Plan assets are recognised at fair value on each reporting date. Changes in fair value — both realised and unrealised — are recognised in the plan's statement of changes in net assets.

## Measurement

Plan investments must be measured at **fair value** (<ParaRef std="IAS 26" para="32" />). This is the single most impactful measurement requirement for Nepali plans:
- **Government securities** — Fair value based on NRB secondary market yields or quoted prices where available.
- **Bank fixed deposits** — Typically approximated at carrying amount if the term is short; longer-term deposits may require fair value adjustment using current market rates.
- **Equity investments** — Quoted shares at NEPSE closing prices; unquoted shares at estimated fair value using income or market approaches (NFRS 13).
- **Real estate** — Independent valuation required. EPF holds significant real estate assets that must be fair-valued annually.

The APVPRB for defined benefit plans is measured using actuarial assumptions: discount rate (government bond yield), salary escalation, mortality, and withdrawal rates. The valuation date should be close to the reporting date, and the actuary's report should be attached or referenced.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

NAS 26 requires the plan's financial statements to include:
- A **statement of net assets available for benefits** — showing investments at fair value, other assets, and liabilities.
- A **statement of changes in net assets** — showing contributions, investment income, benefit payments, and administrative expenses.
- For defined benefit plans: either the APVPRB on the face of the statement or a cross-reference to an actuarial report.
- Significant accounting policies, including the basis for fair value measurement and actuarial methods used.
- Description of the plan, including benefit formula, funding policy, and any changes during the period.
- Investment policy and risk management disclosures.

In Nepal, EPF publishes annual reports with financial statements, but alignment with NAS 26 fair value requirements has been gradual. SSF, being newer, is developing its reporting framework alongside NAS 26 requirements.
