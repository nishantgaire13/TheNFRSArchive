## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Most companies prepare detailed financial statements once a year. But investors and regulators often need information more frequently — every three months (quarterly) or every six months (half-yearly). These shorter-period reports are called "interim financial reports."

NAS 34 sets the minimum content for interim reports. Instead of preparing a full set of financial statements every quarter (which would be expensive and time-consuming), companies can prepare "condensed" versions — shorter reports that highlight what has changed since the last annual report. Think of it as an update, not a complete re-telling.

**Why does this matter in Nepal?** SEBON requires all NEPSE-listed companies to publish quarterly financial reports. NRB requires BFIs to submit quarterly reports. These interim reports move share prices — when Nabil Bank publishes its quarterly results showing higher profits, its share price may jump the next trading day. NAS 34 ensures these reports follow consistent rules.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 34 does not mandate which entities must publish interim reports or how frequently — that is determined by regulators. In Nepal, SEBON requires quarterly reporting for listed entities, and NRB requires quarterly financial reporting for BFIs. What NAS 34 does is prescribe the minimum content when interim reports are issued and the recognition and measurement principles applicable to interim periods.

The standard offers a choice between condensed financial statements and complete financial statements. In practice, most Nepali entities use condensed statements for quarterly filings. Condensed statements include: a condensed balance sheet, condensed statement of profit or loss and OCI, condensed statement of changes in equity, condensed cash flow statement, and selected explanatory notes. The notes should explain events and transactions significant to understanding the changes since the last annual report — not repeat everything from the annual notes.

A critical principle: each interim period should be treated as a discrete reporting period, not merely a "portion" of the annual period. Revenue and expenses are recognised when they occur, not spread artificially over the year. However, NAS 34 contains specific exceptions — for example, income tax expense is based on the estimated effective annual tax rate applied to interim pre-tax income (the "integral" approach to tax).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 34 establishes a discrete-period approach as the general principle (<ParaRef std="IAS 34" para="28" />): recognition and measurement follow the same policies as the annual financial statements. Costs that do not meet the definition of an asset at interim dates are not deferred on the balance sheet simply because they may benefit future interim periods within the same annual cycle. However, the standard contains explicit exceptions that adopt an integral approach for specific items.

The most significant exception is income tax (<ParaRef std="IAS 34" para="B12–B17" />): tax expense is recognised based on the best estimate of the weighted average annual effective tax rate, applied to interim pre-tax income. This integral approach prevents quarter-to-quarter volatility from progressive tax brackets or tax incentive thresholds. In Nepal, the standard 25% corporate rate (30% for BFIs, 20% for certain manufacturing, hydropower during tax holidays) simplifies this — but entities with multiple tax-rate segments (e.g., BFIs with capital gains at different rates) must compute a blended estimated rate.

The materiality assessment for interim periods is evaluated relative to the interim data, not annualised (<ParaRef std="IAS 34" para="23" />). This has implications for error correction and judgement-based recognition thresholds. Seasonality disclosure requirements under <ParaRef std="IAS 34" para="21" /> are relevant for Nepal's tourism sector (peak season Kartik-Falgun), agriculture (harvest-dependent income), and hydropower (monsoon generation peaks). NAS 34 encourages but does not require presentation of rolling twelve-month data for entities with highly seasonal operations.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope & key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 34 applies whenever a company prepares an interim financial report — typically quarterly or half-yearly. The standard itself does not force companies to prepare interim reports; that decision comes from regulators like SEBON or NRB.

**Key terms:**
- **Interim period** — A financial reporting period shorter than a full year (e.g., 3 months or 6 months).
- **Interim financial report** — A financial report for an interim period, containing either condensed or complete financial statements.
- **Condensed financial statements** — Shorter versions of the full financial statements, focusing on changes since the last annual report.
- **Discrete approach** — Treating each quarter as a standalone period for recognising revenue and expenses.
- **Integral approach** — Treating each quarter as part of the full year (used for income tax calculations).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The standard applies to any interim financial report that is described as complying with NFRS. It prescribes minimum content but does not restrict entities from providing additional information. Compliance with NAS 34 must be disclosed in the interim report (<ParaRef std="IAS 34" para="19" />).

Key definitions:
- **Interim period** — a financial reporting period shorter than a full financial year.
- **Condensed financial statements** — include, at minimum, each of the headings and sub-totals in the most recent annual financial statements, plus selected explanatory notes.
- **Comparative periods** — the balance sheet compares to the end of the most recent annual period; profit or loss compares to the same interim period of the prior year (e.g., Q1 of this year vs Q1 of last year).
- **Significant events and transactions** — the focus of interim notes: business combinations, restructuring, changes in estimates, debt and equity issuances, segment revenue and results if NFRS 8 applies annually.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

Scope clarification: NAS 34 does not define what constitutes an "interim financial report" — if an entity publishes financial information for an interim period but does not describe it as complying with NFRS, NAS 34 does not technically apply. However, SEBON's listing regulations require quarterly reports to comply with NFRS, which triggers NAS 34 applicability. NRB's quarterly templates for BFIs are regulatory returns rather than general-purpose financial reports — a grey area, though best practice is NAS 34 compliance. The interaction with NFRS 8 segment reporting is noteworthy: if NFRS 8 applies in annual statements, segment information is required in interim reports using the same segments (<ParaRef std="IAS 34" para="16A(g)" />). Impairment reversals recognised at an interim date under NAS 36/NFRS 9 are not reversed simply because the annual assessment yields a different conclusion — each interim period's assessment stands.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

The same recognition and measurement policies used in annual financial statements apply to interim periods (<ParaRef std="IAS 34" para="28" />). Revenue received seasonally or occasionally is not anticipated or deferred at interim dates unless it would be appropriate at the annual date. Costs incurred unevenly during the year are anticipated or deferred only if it is also appropriate to anticipate or defer at year-end. This discrete-period approach prevents manipulation through artificial smoothing. The key exception is income tax: interim tax expense uses the estimated effective annual rate, spreading the annual tax burden proportionally across interim periods (<ParaRef std="IAS 34" para="B12" />).

## Measurement

Measurements for interim reporting are made on a year-to-date basis, with the frequency of reporting not affecting the measurement of annual results (<ParaRef std="IAS 34" para="28" />). Inventories are measured at NRV at each interim date — write-downs are not deferred to year-end. Foreign currency translation gains and losses follow the same principles as annual reporting. Pension costs use the estimated annual rate, prorated. Contingencies and provisions are assessed at each interim date using the same criteria as annual assessments. The estimated annual effective tax rate is applied to interim pre-tax income; discrete items (e.g., changes in tax law enacted during the interim period) are recognised in the interim period of enactment.

## Presentation & disclosure

Condensed interim financial statements include, at minimum, the headings and sub-totals from the most recent annual statements, selected explanatory notes for significant events, basic and diluted EPS, and comparative statements as required by the standard (<ParaRef std="IAS 34" para="8–14" />). Selected notes must cover changes in accounting policies, seasonality or cyclicality of operations, unusual items, changes in estimates, issuances or repurchases of debt and equity, dividends paid, segment information, events after the interim period, changes in business composition, and changes in contingent liabilities or assets. In Nepal, SEBON prescribes additional quarterly disclosure formats that supplement NAS 34 requirements, including sector-specific ratios for BFIs and real-sector entities.
