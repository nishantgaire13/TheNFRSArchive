## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Sometimes companies reward their employees not just with cash salaries but with shares or options to buy shares. Imagine a growing fintech startup in Kathmandu offering its software developers the right to buy company shares at today's price after working there for three years. If the company grows and its share price rises, those developers benefit — it is like getting a bonus that grows with the company's success. NFRS 2 tells companies how to record these share-based payments in their books.

In Nepal, share-based payments are still relatively uncommon compared to traditional cash bonuses. However, they are emerging in the tech sector, fintech companies, and among BFI promoter groups. When a listed company on NEPSE grants shares to its employees or directors, NFRS 2 requires the company to recognise an expense even though no cash leaves the business. This ensures that investors see the true cost of compensating employees.

The basic idea is simple: if you give someone something valuable — whether shares, options, or cash linked to share prices — you must record the cost. The standard ensures that a company like NIC Asia or Global IME cannot grant thousands of share options to executives without showing any expense in its profit and loss statement.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 2 establishes recognition and measurement principles for three categories of share-based payment transactions: equity-settled (the entity issues shares or options), cash-settled (the entity pays cash based on the price of its shares), and transactions with cash alternatives (where either party can choose settlement in shares or cash). The standard requires entities to reflect the effects of share-based payment transactions in profit or loss and on the balance sheet.

For equity-settled transactions, the entity measures goods or services received — and the corresponding increase in equity — at the fair value of the goods or services received, unless that fair value cannot be estimated reliably, in which case the entity uses the fair value of the equity instruments granted. Employee share options are always measured at the grant-date fair value of the instruments because employee services cannot be reliably measured directly.

In Nepal, the practical challenge is fair value measurement. NEPSE-listed shares have observable market prices, but option valuation models (Black-Scholes, binomial) require inputs such as expected volatility, dividend yield, and risk-free rate that are difficult to estimate in Nepal's relatively thin and volatile market. BFI promoter share schemes and fintech ESOP plans are the most common transactions requiring NFRS 2 application.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NFRS 2 requires grant-date fair value measurement for equity-settled transactions with employees (<ParaRef std="IFRS 2" para="11" />), with no subsequent remeasurement of the equity component — changes in fair value after grant date do not affect the expense. This contrasts sharply with cash-settled transactions, which require remeasurement at each reporting date until settlement (<ParaRef std="IFRS 2" para="30" />), creating ongoing profit or loss volatility.

The treatment of vesting conditions is critical: market conditions (e.g., share price targets) are incorporated into grant-date fair value and are not subsequently adjusted — even if the market condition is never met, the expense is still recognised over the vesting period (<ParaRef std="IFRS 2" para="21" />). Non-market performance conditions (e.g., profit targets, loan recovery ratios for BFIs) and service conditions affect the number of instruments expected to vest, with true-up adjustments at each reporting date.

Nepal presents unique challenges: the absence of a liquid options market means there is no observable option pricing benchmark; NEPSE's circuit breaker mechanisms and thin trading volumes make historical volatility estimates unreliable; and the limited pool of qualified valuation professionals means many entities struggle with Black-Scholes or lattice model implementation. NRB has not issued specific guidance on BFI share-based payment schemes, leaving entities to apply NFRS 2 without sector-specific regulatory overlay.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NFRS 2 covers any transaction where a company receives goods or services and pays for them using shares, share options, or cash amounts linked to the company's share price. It does **not** cover shares issued in a business combination (that falls under NFRS 3) or certain financial instrument contracts within the scope of NFRS 9.

**Key terms:**
- **Share-based payment** — A transaction where the entity receives goods or services as consideration for its equity instruments, or by incurring liabilities based on the price of its shares.
- **Equity-settled** — The entity pays by issuing its own shares or share options.
- **Cash-settled** — The entity pays cash, but the amount is based on the share price.
- **Vesting period** — The time an employee must wait (usually while continuing to work) before the shares or options become theirs.
- **Grant date** — The date the company and the employee agree on the terms of the share-based payment.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 2 applies to all share-based payment transactions, whether the entity can identify specifically the goods or services received or not (<ParaRef std="IFRS 2" para="2" />). It encompasses transactions with employees and non-employees (e.g., consultants, advisors). Exclusions include shares issued in business combinations under NFRS 3, and contracts to buy or sell non-financial items within the scope of NFRS 9.

Key technical definitions:
- **Fair value** — The amount for which an asset could be exchanged, a liability settled, or an equity instrument granted could be exchanged, between knowledgeable, willing parties in an arm's length transaction (<ParaRef std="IFRS 2" para="A" />).
- **Vesting conditions** — Conditions that must be satisfied for the counterparty to become entitled to receive cash, other assets, or equity instruments. Includes service conditions, performance conditions (market and non-market), and combinations thereof.
- **Modification** — A change to the terms and conditions on which equity instruments were granted, including repricing, changes in vesting conditions, or cancellation and replacement.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope boundary between NFRS 2 and NFRS 9 requires careful analysis. A contract to deliver a fixed number of own equity instruments for a fixed amount of cash is an equity instrument under NAS 32 and outside NFRS 2. However, if the number of instruments varies with the entity's share price, or if there is a cash-settlement alternative, the transaction may fall partly under NFRS 2 and partly under NFRS 9 (<ParaRef std="IFRS 2" para="6" />).

Edge cases relevant to Nepal include: (a) promoter share allocation schemes in BFIs where promoters receive additional shares at par value as a reward — these may constitute share-based payments if there is an identifiable service component; (b) share-based payments to non-employees such as legal advisors or underwriters who accept shares instead of fees — measured at fair value of services received if reliably measurable, otherwise at fair value of equity instruments granted; and (c) group share-based payment arrangements where a parent entity (e.g., a BFI holding company) grants options over its shares to subsidiary employees — requiring careful allocation between parent and subsidiary financial statements (<ParaRef std="IFRS 2" para="43A-43D" />).

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

NFRS 2 requires the entity to recognise the goods or services received in a share-based payment transaction when it obtains the goods or as the services are received. For equity-settled transactions, the entity recognises a corresponding increase in equity; for cash-settled transactions, it recognises a liability. The expense is recognised over the vesting period — the period during which all specified vesting conditions must be satisfied.

In Nepal, most share-based payment arrangements involve employee service conditions (typically 2-5 years of continuous employment). The entity recognises the expense on a straight-line basis over the vesting period, adjusting at each reporting date for expected forfeitures based on non-market vesting conditions. For a NEPSE-listed company granting 10,000 options with a grant-date fair value of NPR 150 each and a 3-year vesting period, the annual expense would be approximately NPR 5,00,000 (before forfeiture adjustments).

## Measurement

For equity-settled transactions with employees, the entity measures the fair value of equity instruments at grant date and does not subsequently remeasure. Option pricing models — Black-Scholes-Merton or binomial lattice — are used when no observable market price exists for the options. Key inputs include the exercise price, current share price, expected volatility (derived from NEPSE historical price data), expected dividends, risk-free interest rate (typically Nepal government bond yields), and the expected option life.

For cash-settled transactions, the entity measures the liability at fair value at each reporting date until settlement, with changes recognised in profit or loss. In Nepal, cash-settled share-based payments are less common but may arise in phantom share schemes or share appreciation rights used by unlisted companies that cannot issue equity easily. The ongoing remeasurement creates earnings volatility, particularly given NEPSE's price fluctuations.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

NFRS 2 requires disclosure of information that enables users to understand the nature and extent of share-based payment arrangements, how the fair value of goods or services received (or equity instruments granted) was determined, and the effect of share-based payment transactions on the entity's profit or loss and financial position.

Key disclosures include: a description of each type of arrangement (including general terms and conditions), the number and weighted average exercise prices of share options outstanding and exercisable, the range of exercise prices and weighted average remaining contractual life, the fair value measurement method and inputs used (model, volatility, dividend yield, risk-free rate), and the total expense recognised in profit or loss. For Nepal, entities must also consider NRB and SEBON disclosure requirements that may supplement NFRS 2, particularly for listed BFIs where share-based compensation may affect capital adequacy calculations.
