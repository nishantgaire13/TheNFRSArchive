## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Not all valuable things in a business are physical. A company's software, brand name, patent, or licence can be worth crores — but you cannot touch them. These are **intangible assets**.

NAS 38 tells businesses when they can record intangible assets on their balance sheet and how to value them over time. The key rule is that you can only recognise an intangible asset if it is **identifiable** (you can separate it from the business or it arises from a legal right), it is **controlled** by the entity, and it will generate **future economic benefits**.

For example, when Nepal Telecom buys spectrum from NTA for NPR 50 crore, that licence is an intangible asset — it is identifiable, controlled by Nepal Telecom, and generates future revenue. But Nepal Telecom's reputation for reliability? That cannot be recognised as an asset because it is not separable and cannot be measured reliably.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 38 prescribes the accounting treatment for intangible assets — non-monetary assets without physical substance that are identifiable (either separable or arising from contractual/legal rights). The standard addresses initial recognition (acquisition, internal generation, business combination), subsequent measurement (cost model or revaluation model), amortisation, and impairment.

A critical distinction is between **research** costs (always expensed) and **development** costs (capitalised only when six strict criteria are met). This is highly relevant for Nepal's growing IT sector and banking industry investing in core banking systems. Another key area is telecom licences from NTA, hydropower licences, and software assets — all common intangible assets in Nepali entities.

NAS 38 is fully aligned with IAS 38. Goodwill acquired in a business combination is excluded — it is covered by NFRS 3 and tested for impairment under NAS 36.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 38 establishes a recognition hurdle that is deliberately higher for internally generated intangibles than for purchased ones — reflecting the difficulty of reliably measuring internally generated value. The identifiability criterion (<ParaRef std="IAS 38" para="12" />) and the prohibition on recognising internally generated goodwill, brands, mastheads, customer lists, and similar items (<ParaRef std="IAS 38" para="63-64" />) create an asymmetry: an acquired customer list is recognised at fair value in a business combination under NFRS 3, but an organically developed customer list is never recognised.

The research-development boundary (<ParaRef std="IAS 38" para="54-62" />) requires six cumulative criteria for capitalisation of development costs: technical feasibility, intention to complete, ability to use or sell, probable future economic benefits, availability of resources, and reliable measurement. In practice, Nepali IT firms and banks investing in proprietary software must establish clear project milestones to evidence the transition from research to development.

The interaction with NAS 36 is critical — intangible assets with indefinite useful lives (e.g., perpetual licences) are not amortised but tested annually for impairment. Assets not yet available for use are also tested annually. Nepal's licensing regime, where telecom spectrum and hydropower licences have defined terms, typically results in finite-life classification with systematic amortisation.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 38 covers intangible assets like software, patents, trademarks, and licences. It does NOT cover: financial assets, mineral rights, insurance contracts, goodwill from acquisitions (that is NFRS 3), or intangible assets covered by other standards.

**Key terms:**
- **Intangible asset** — A non-physical asset you can identify separately, that your business controls, and that will bring future benefits.
- **Amortisation** — Spreading the cost of an intangible asset over its useful life, similar to depreciation for physical assets.
- **Research** — Original investigation to gain new knowledge. Always an expense.
- **Development** — Applying research findings to a plan for a new product or process. Can be capitalised if six conditions are met.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The standard applies to all intangible assets except those within the scope of other standards: financial assets (NFRS 9), exploration assets (NFRS 6), mineral rights, insurance contracts, goodwill from business combinations (NFRS 3), deferred tax assets (NAS 12), lease assets (NFRS 16), and employee benefit assets (NAS 19) (<ParaRef std="IAS 38" para="2-3" />).

- **Identifiable** — separable (can be sold, licensed, transferred) or arises from contractual or legal rights (<ParaRef std="IAS 38" para="12" />).
- **Useful life** — finite (amortised) or indefinite (not amortised, tested annually for impairment).
- **Active market** — required for the revaluation model (rare for intangibles — taxi licences and fishing quotas are textbook examples, not common in Nepal).
- **Internally generated intangible** — must pass the research/development boundary test. Website development costs, core banking software projects, and proprietary ERP systems are common Nepal examples.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope interaction with NFRS 3 is particularly important: in a business combination, acquirer recognises intangible assets of the acquiree separately from goodwill if they meet the identifiability criterion — even if the acquiree had not recognised them. This creates significant fair value measurement challenges in Nepali M&A transactions, especially NRB-directed bank mergers where the acquiree's customer relationships, core deposits, and brand may have substantial value but limited market evidence for measurement.

Bearer plants were moved from NAS 41 to NAS 16 in 2016, but the biological produce growing on bearer plants remains under NAS 41. This distinction matters for Nepali tea estates — the tea bushes (bearer plants) are PPE under NAS 16, not intangible assets under NAS 38, even though they have characteristics of both.

The revaluation model is theoretically available but requires an active market (<ParaRef std="IAS 38" para="75" />). In Nepal, no active market exists for most intangible asset classes, making the revaluation model effectively unavailable. Entities default to the cost model with amortisation over estimated useful life.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

An intangible asset is recognised when it is probable that expected future economic benefits will flow to the entity and the cost can be measured reliably (<ParaRef std="IAS 38" para="21" />). Separately acquired intangibles always meet both criteria — the purchase price is the reliable cost measure and the act of purchasing evidences management's expectation of future benefits.

For internally generated intangibles, all research phase costs are expensed as incurred. Development costs are capitalised only when all six criteria are simultaneously met (<ParaRef std="IAS 38" para="57" />): technical feasibility, intention to complete, ability to use or sell, demonstration of probable future economic benefits, adequate resources, and ability to measure expenditure reliably. Costs previously expensed in research cannot be retrospectively capitalised.

## Measurement

At initial recognition, intangible assets are measured at cost. For separately acquired assets, cost includes purchase price plus directly attributable costs of preparing the asset for use. In a business combination, cost is fair value at acquisition date. For internally generated assets, cost is the sum of expenditures from the date the development criteria are first met (<ParaRef std="IAS 38" para="65" />).

After initial recognition, entities choose either the cost model (cost less accumulated amortisation and impairment) or the revaluation model (fair value less subsequent amortisation and impairment — requires active market). Finite-life intangibles are amortised over their useful life using a method reflecting the pattern of consumption. Indefinite-life intangibles are not amortised but tested annually for impairment under NAS 36.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

Entities must disclose for each class of intangible asset: useful lives (finite or indefinite), amortisation methods and rates, gross carrying amount and accumulated amortisation/impairment at beginning and end of period, and the line item in the statement of comprehensive income in which amortisation is included (<ParaRef std="IAS 38" para="118" />).

Additional disclosures include: the carrying amount of intangible assets with indefinite useful lives and reasons supporting the indefinite assessment, any individually material intangible assets (description, carrying amount, remaining amortisation period), assets acquired through government grants, and restrictions on title or pledged assets. Research and development expenditure recognised as an expense during the period must also be disclosed.
