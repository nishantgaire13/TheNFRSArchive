## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Nepal imports most of its fuel, vehicles, machinery, and consumer goods from abroad. Every time a Nepali company buys goods priced in US dollars, euros, or Indian rupees, the exchange rate can change between when the deal is made and when payment is actually sent. NAS 21 tells companies how to handle these exchange rate changes in their accounts.

Think of it like this: Surya Nepal agrees to buy raw materials for USD 1,00,000 when the rate is NPR 134 per dollar (total NPR 1,34,00,000). By the time they pay two months later, the rate has moved to NPR 136 per dollar (total NPR 1,36,00,000). The extra NPR 2,00,000 is an "exchange loss" that must be recorded in the accounts.

**Why does this matter?** Nepal's economy is heavily exposed to foreign currency — from remittances (over NPR 1 lakh crore annually) to import bills. Getting exchange rate accounting right directly affects a company's reported profits.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 21 prescribes how to include foreign currency transactions in an entity's financial statements and how to translate the financial statements of a foreign operation for inclusion in consolidated statements. It covers initial recognition, subsequent reporting dates, and settlement of foreign currency monetary items.

For Nepali entities, the standard is particularly significant because: (a) NPR is pegged to the Indian Rupee at 1.6:1 — meaning INR transactions have minimal exchange risk but USD/EUR/GBP transactions carry full volatility, (b) NRB sets daily exchange rates that entities must reference, (c) foreign currency loans are common for large infrastructure projects, especially hydropower, and (d) remittance-receiving financial institutions handle massive FX volumes. The functional currency for virtually all Nepali entities is NPR, since that is the currency of the primary economic environment in which they operate.

NAS 21 is fully aligned with IAS 21 — no carve-outs exist.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 21's conceptual foundation rests on the functional currency concept (<ParaRef std="IAS 21" para="9" />) — the currency of the primary economic environment in which the entity operates. This is a factual determination, not a policy choice. For Nepali entities, functional currency is almost invariably NPR, but edge cases arise: a wholly export-oriented carpet manufacturer whose revenues, pricing, and key costs are denominated in USD may have USD as its functional currency despite being domiciled in Nepal.

The standard's dual treatment — transaction gains/losses in P&L (<ParaRef std="IAS 21" para="28" />) versus translation differences on foreign operations in OCI (<ParaRef std="IAS 21" para="39" />) — creates complexity for Nepali groups with overseas subsidiaries or branches. The NPR-INR peg at 1.6:1 means Indian operations face near-zero translation risk, but NRB's periodic adjustments to the peg (or market pressure on it) could trigger material reclassification from OCI to P&L on disposal.

Net investment hedging under NAS 21 read with NFRS 9 is theoretically available but practically rare in Nepal given the underdeveloped derivatives market. The interaction with NAS 23 (Borrowing Costs) for foreign currency borrowings creates a particularly complex area: exchange differences on the principal of foreign currency loans for qualifying assets are expensed, not capitalised, creating divergence from some practitioners' expectations.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope & key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 21 covers:
- **Foreign currency transactions** — any purchase, sale, or loan in a currency other than NPR
- **Translating foreign operations** — converting the accounts of a foreign branch or subsidiary into NPR

**Not covered:** Derivative instruments used to hedge foreign currency risk (those fall under NFRS 9).

**Key terms:**
- **Functional currency** — the main currency the business operates in. For most Nepali companies, this is NPR.
- **Presentation currency** — the currency used to present financial statements (also NPR for Nepali entities).
- **Exchange rate** — the rate at which one currency is exchanged for another. NRB publishes daily rates.
- **Monetary items** — cash and items to be received or paid in fixed amounts of money (receivables, payables, loans).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The standard applies to accounting for transactions in foreign currencies, translating results and financial position of foreign operations, and translating an entity's results into a different presentation currency. It does not apply to hedge accounting for foreign currency items (NFRS 9) or to the presentation of cash flows from foreign currency transactions (NAS 7).

Key definitions:
- **Functional currency** — the currency of the primary economic environment in which the entity operates, determined by primary indicators (revenue currency, cost currency) and secondary indicators (financing, operating receipts).
- **Closing rate** — the spot exchange rate at the end of the reporting period.
- **Spot rate** — the exchange rate for immediate delivery.
- **Monetary items** — units of currency held and assets/liabilities to be received/paid in fixed or determinable amounts. Non-monetary items (PPE, inventory, intangibles) are not retranslated after initial recognition at cost.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

Scope interactions: NAS 21 applies to foreign currency derivatives that are not within the hedge accounting scope of NFRS 9. The standard's treatment of exchange differences on monetary items forming part of a net investment in a foreign operation (<ParaRef std="IAS 21" para="32" />) creates an OCI classification that survives until disposal — recycling through P&L on derecognition of the foreign operation. In Nepal, this is relevant for banks with foreign branches (e.g., Nabil Bank's operations) and for Nepali conglomerates with Indian or other SAARC subsidiaries.

The functional currency assessment for entities operating in Nepal's Special Economic Zones (SEZs) requires careful analysis: revenue may be predominantly in USD (export-oriented), but labour costs, local procurement, and financing are in NPR. The weight of evidence typically points to NPR, but not always. For groups, each entity within the group determines its own functional currency independently (<ParaRef std="IAS 21" para="11" />).

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

A foreign currency transaction is initially recorded at the functional currency spot rate at the date of the transaction (<ParaRef std="IAS 21" para="21" />). At each subsequent reporting date, monetary items are retranslated at the closing rate, non-monetary items measured at historical cost remain at the transaction date rate, and non-monetary items measured at fair value are translated at the rate when fair value was determined (<ParaRef std="IAS 21" para="23" />). Exchange differences on monetary items are recognised in profit or loss in the period they arise (<ParaRef std="IAS 21" para="28" />).

## Measurement

For translating a foreign operation's financial statements into the parent's presentation currency, assets and liabilities are translated at the closing rate, income and expenses at transaction date rates (or average rate as a practical expedient), and resulting exchange differences are recognised in OCI (<ParaRef std="IAS 21" para="39" />). Goodwill and fair value adjustments arising on acquisition of a foreign operation are treated as assets and liabilities of the foreign operation and translated at the closing rate. On disposal of a foreign operation, the cumulative exchange differences in OCI are reclassified to profit or loss (<ParaRef std="IAS 21" para="48" />).

## Presentation & disclosure

Entities must disclose the amount of exchange differences recognised in profit or loss (excluding differences on financial instruments at FVTPL), net exchange differences recognised in OCI and accumulated in a separate component of equity, and a reconciliation of opening and closing balances of the exchange differences component (<ParaRef std="IAS 21" para="52" />). When the presentation currency differs from the functional currency, or when the functional currency changes, the reason must be disclosed.
