## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Some businesses — like electricity companies and telecom operators — do not set their own prices. Instead, a **government regulator** decides how much they can charge customers. This is called **rate regulation**.

For example, the **Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA)** cannot charge whatever it wants for electricity. The tariff is set by the **Nepal Electricity Regulatory Commission (NERC)** based on NEA's costs plus a reasonable return. If NEA spends more than expected this year (say, due to a drought reducing hydropower output), the regulator may allow NEA to **recover that extra cost in future tariffs** — charging customers a little more next year to make up the difference.

Under regular accounting rules, that extra cost would just be an expense this year — gone. But with rate regulation, NEA knows it will recover the cost through future tariffs. NFRS 14 says: if you are a **first-time NFRS adopter** and you previously recognised these "recoverable costs" as an asset on your balance sheet under your old accounting rules, you can **continue** doing so when you switch to NFRS.

This is a **narrow, interim standard**. It only applies to entities adopting NFRS for the first time, and only if they have rate-regulated activities. In Nepal, this primarily affects **NEA** and **Nepal Telecom (NTC)**.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 14 permits a **first-time adopter** of NFRS (applying NFRS 1) that conducts **rate-regulated activities** to continue recognising amounts related to rate regulation (**regulatory deferral account balances**) that were recognised in the entity's previous GAAP financial statements. This is an interim measure pending the IASB's comprehensive rate-regulated activities project.

**Rate regulation** exists when a government body (the rate regulator) establishes the price or tariff that an entity can charge customers for goods or services. The price is designed to recover the entity's allowable costs and provide a return. Key characteristics:
- The tariff is set by a **body empowered by statute** (not a contractual arrangement)
- The tariff-setting process is designed to **recover the entity's costs** of providing goods/services
- The tariff includes a **return** on investment (regulated rate of return)

**Regulatory deferral account balances** arise when the regulator allows or requires the entity to defer costs (or revenue) to/from future periods. They come in two forms:
- **Regulatory deferral account debit balances (assets)** — Costs incurred now that the regulator has allowed to be recovered through future tariffs. Example: NEA incurs NPR 5 billion in extraordinary maintenance costs; NERC allows recovery over 5 years through tariff surcharges.
- **Regulatory deferral account credit balances (liabilities)** — Revenue collected now that must be refunded or offset in future periods. Example: NEA overcharged due to an interim tariff that exceeded final approved rates; the excess must be returned through reduced future tariffs.

**Scope limitations:**
- Only available to **first-time NFRS adopters** — entities already reporting under NFRS cannot begin applying NFRS 14
- The entity must have recognised regulatory deferral balances under its **previous GAAP**
- Cannot be applied by analogy to other situations

In Nepal, NEA transitioned from Nepal Accounting Standards (based on older Indian GAAP) to NFRS, and NFRS 14 was critical in allowing NEA to continue recognising regulatory assets and liabilities that would otherwise have been derecognised under general NFRS principles (since no other NFRS specifically permits recognition of such items).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NFRS 14 represents one of the IASB's most politically charged compromises. The fundamental issue: under general NFRS principles, regulatory deferral account balances do not meet the Conceptual Framework's definitions of assets and liabilities (pre-2018 Framework). A "right to charge higher prices in the future" is not a resource controlled by the entity — it depends on future regulatory decisions, future customer consumption, and the entity's continued existence as a regulated entity. Yet derecognising these balances on NFRS adoption would dramatically distort the entity's financial position and potentially undermine the regulatory compact.

The IASB's solution was deliberately narrow: NFRS 14 applies **only** to first-time adopters, preserving the status quo without expanding regulatory accounting to entities already on NFRS. This created a two-tier system that the IASB acknowledged was unsatisfactory — hence the ongoing comprehensive project (now IFRS 18-adjacent, with an exposure draft expected).

**Nepal's rate-regulated entities:**

**Nepal Electricity Authority (NEA):** NEA operates under the Nepal Electricity Authority Act 2041 (1984) and is subject to tariff regulation by NERC (established under the Electricity Regulatory Commission Act 2074). NEA's regulatory deferral balances include:
- **Deferred fuel costs:** When NEA purchases electricity from thermal/diesel plants at costs exceeding the tariff-embedded fuel rate, the excess is deferred for future recovery. Nepal's growing reliance on cross-border power imports from India introduces exchange rate risk into fuel cost recovery.
- **Deferred system loss costs:** NERC allows a target system loss percentage (currently ~15.3% for NEA). Actual losses exceeding the target may be partially deferred if attributable to infrastructure investment gaps rather than inefficiency.
- **Capital expenditure recovery:** NEA's massive hydropower investment programme (Melamchi, Upper Tamakoshi) creates regulatory assets for costs that will be recovered through tariffs over the asset's useful life. The timing mismatch between capital spending and tariff recovery is a primary source of regulatory deferral balances.
- **Foreign exchange adjustments:** Power purchase agreements with Indian generators (e.g., from NHPC cross-border lines) are denominated in INR. Exchange rate movements between NPR and INR create cost variances that NERC may allow recovery through tariff adjustments.

**Nepal Telecom (NTC):** NTC operates under the Telecommunications Act 2053 and is regulated by the Nepal Telecommunications Authority (NTA). Regulatory deferral balances include universal service obligations (costs of extending service to uneconomic rural areas, partially recoverable through the Rural Telecommunications Development Fund) and spectrum licence amortisation recovery through tariffs.

**Measurement and presentation:**
NFRS 14 requires regulatory deferral account balances to be presented as **separate line items** in the statement of financial position — not commingled with other assets/liabilities. Similarly, net movements in regulatory deferral accounts are presented as **separate line items** in the statement of profit or loss and OCI, after the subtotal of profit or loss (i.e., below the line, as a separate section). This presentation ensures that users can identify and, if they choose, remove the effect of rate regulation from the financial statements.

Impairment of regulatory deferral account debit balances is assessed when indicators suggest that the regulator may not allow full recovery — for example, if NERC signals a policy shift away from cost-plus regulation toward incentive-based regulation, or if a regulatory review disallows certain costs. The impairment charge is presented within the separate regulatory deferral line items, not in regular operating expenses.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NFRS 14 has a very narrow scope:
- Only for entities **adopting NFRS for the first time** (not for entities already using NFRS)
- Only for entities with **rate-regulated activities** — where a government body sets their prices
- Only if the entity **already recognised** regulatory deferral balances under its old accounting rules

**Key terms:**
- **Rate regulation** — A framework where a government regulator sets the price an entity can charge. Example: NERC sets electricity tariffs for NEA.
- **Rate regulator** — The government body with authority to set prices. Example: NERC for electricity, NTA for telecommunications.
- **Regulatory deferral account balance** — An amount on the balance sheet that would not be recognised under other NFRS, but exists because the regulator allows cost recovery through future prices.
- **First-time adopter** — An entity presenting its first NFRS financial statements (applying NFRS 1).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The scope is deliberately restrictive. An entity may apply NFRS 14 if, and only if, it:
1. Is a first-time adopter of NFRS (per NFRS 1)
2. Conducts rate-regulated activities
3. Recognised regulatory deferral account balances in its previous GAAP financial statements for those activities

An entity with both regulated and unregulated activities applies NFRS 14 only to the rate-regulated portion. If only some activities are rate-regulated (e.g., an energy company with regulated distribution and unregulated generation), the entity applies NFRS 14 to regulatory deferral balances related to the regulated activities.

**Rate-regulated activity** is not defined by a bright-line test. The standard looks at substance: is there a body empowered by statute that establishes prices designed to recover the entity's allowable costs and earn a regulated return? Contractual pricing arrangements (even with government counterparties) are generally not rate regulation unless the pricing body has statutory authority.

Subsidiary considerations: if a subsidiary applies NFRS 14 in its own financial statements, the parent can continue presenting those regulatory deferral balances in consolidated financial statements. However, if the parent itself was already an NFRS reporter, the parent cannot start recognising new regulatory deferral balances — it can only continue those inherited through subsidiary consolidation.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The interaction between NFRS 14 and NFRS 1 (First-time Adoption) is precise. NFRS 1 generally requires retrospective application of all NFRS, with specific exemptions and exceptions. NFRS 14 provides an additional exemption: the entity may continue its previous GAAP accounting policy for regulatory deferral balances, including the classification and measurement. This means the entity does not need to assess whether regulatory deferral balances meet the NFRS Conceptual Framework's asset/liability definitions — a deliberate carve-out from the framework's requirements.

The "previous GAAP" anchor is critical: the entity can only continue balances that were recognised under previous GAAP. It cannot create new regulatory deferral balances that were not recognised before. If NEA's previous GAAP did not recognise a particular type of regulatory deferral (e.g., because the accounting policy was conservative), NEA cannot begin recognising it just because NERC permits it. Conversely, if previous GAAP recognised a balance that NERC subsequently disallowed, the entity must derecognise it (an impairment event).

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

Regulatory deferral account debit balances are recognised when the entity has a right to charge a higher price in the future as a result of costs incurred in the current or prior periods that are permitted by the regulator for future recovery. Regulatory deferral account credit balances are recognised when the entity has collected amounts from customers that the regulator requires to be returned through lower future prices.

Changes in regulatory deferral account balances are recognised in each reporting period to reflect: new amounts deferred (new costs incurred that will be recovered, or new amounts collected that must be returned), amounts recovered or returned through current-period tariffs, and any impairment losses.

An entity ceases to recognise a regulatory deferral account debit balance when the regulator no longer permits recovery, the entity no longer conducts the rate-regulated activity, or there is no longer a basis for expecting the amount to be recoverable through future tariffs.

## Measurement

Regulatory deferral account balances are measured at the amount recognised under previous GAAP, adjusted each period for new amounts deferred, amounts realised (recovered or returned), and impairment.

There is no fair value option — NFRS 14 does not require or permit fair value measurement. Balances are measured at their regulatory-determined amounts, which reflect the cost basis as approved by the regulator.

Impairment is recognised when there are indicators that the regulatory deferral account debit balance is no longer fully recoverable. Indicators include: adverse regulatory decisions, policy changes by the regulator, significant decline in customer demand, entry of competitors (for partially deregulated markets), and changes in legislation affecting the regulatory framework.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

NFRS 14 has specific presentation requirements designed to ensure transparency:

**Statement of financial position:**
- Regulatory deferral account debit balances presented as a **single separate line item** after total assets
- Regulatory deferral account credit balances presented as a **single separate line item** after total liabilities
- Not classified as current/non-current (although the entity may voluntarily provide this breakdown in notes)

**Statement of profit or loss and OCI:**
- Net movement in regulatory deferral account balances presented as **separate line items** after the subtotal of profit or loss
- A separate subtotal showing "profit or loss and net movement in regulatory deferral account balances"

**Disclosures include:**
- Nature and extent of rate regulation, including the rate-setting process and regulatory body
- Description of each type of regulatory deferral account balance
- Amounts recognised, derecognised, and recovered during the period
- Rate of return permitted by the regulator
- Remaining recovery or reversal period
- Risks associated with future recovery
