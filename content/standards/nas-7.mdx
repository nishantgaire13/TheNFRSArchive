## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

A company's profit and loss statement tells you whether it made a profit, but it does not tell you whether it actually has cash in the bank. A business can be profitable on paper but still run out of money if customers are slow to pay. The statement of cash flows solves this problem — it tracks every rupee that came in and went out during the year.

Think of it like a household budget: you know your salary (cash in from operations), you know you bought a motorbike on loan (investing and financing), and at the end of the month you check your bank balance. NAS 7 requires companies to do exactly this — but in a structured format.

Cash flows are split into three buckets: **operating activities** (day-to-day business — selling goods, paying suppliers, paying salaries), **investing activities** (buying or selling long-term assets like land, machinery, or shares in other companies), and **financing activities** (borrowing money, repaying loans, issuing shares, paying dividends). By reading a cash flow statement, an investor can see whether Nepal Telecom generates enough cash from its phone services to fund new tower installations without needing more loans.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 7 requires all entities to present a statement of cash flows as an integral part of their financial statements. The statement classifies cash flows during the period into operating, investing, and financing activities. This classification provides information that allows users to assess the impact of each activity on the entity's financial position and its cash and cash equivalents.

For operating activities, entities may use the **direct method** (showing gross cash receipts and payments) or the **indirect method** (adjusting profit or loss for non-cash items, working capital changes, and items classified as investing or financing). NAS 7 encourages the direct method, but in practice, virtually all Nepali entities use the indirect method due to its simplicity.

Nepal-specific considerations are significant for BFIs: NRB's Unified Directives require specific cash flow formats for banks and financial institutions. Interest received and paid by BFIs are classified as operating activities (their core business), whereas manufacturing companies may classify interest paid as either operating or financing.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 7 implements a cash-basis complement to the accrual-basis financial statements required by NAS 1. The statement eliminates the effects of differing accounting policies for the same transactions (e.g., depreciation methods, revenue timing) and enables cross-entity comparability on a pure liquidity basis.

The classification flexibility in <ParaRef std="IAS 7" para="31-34" /> regarding interest and dividends creates structuring opportunities. Interest paid may be classified as operating (consistent with its deduction in arriving at profit) or financing (cost of obtaining financial resources). Dividends received may be operating (return on investments) or investing (return of investments). Once chosen, the classification must be applied consistently. Nepal's banking sector uniformly classifies interest paid/received as operating — consistent with <ParaRef std="IAS 7" para="33" />'s allowance for financial institutions.

The indirect method reconciliation (<ParaRef std="IAS 7" para="18-20" />) serves as a powerful analytical tool but obscures gross cash flows. In Nepal, the direct method's limited adoption is partly due to accounting systems not being configured to capture gross cash data — most ERP implementations in Nepal (Tally, custom systems) default to indirect method outputs. The IASB has periodically considered mandating the direct method but has not done so.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 7 applies to **all entities** preparing financial statements under NFRS — from small trading companies to Nepal's largest banks. Every business must include a cash flow statement.

**Key terms:**
- **Cash** — Money in hand and deposits withdrawable on demand (your bank balance).
- **Cash equivalents** — Short-term, highly liquid investments easily convertible to known amounts of cash with insignificant risk of value change (e.g., a 3-month fixed deposit at Nabil Bank).
- **Operating activities** — The main revenue-producing activities of the entity (selling goods, providing services).
- **Investing activities** — Buying and selling long-term assets (land, buildings, equipment, investments in other companies).
- **Financing activities** — Activities that change the size and composition of equity and borrowings (taking loans, issuing shares, paying dividends).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 7 applies to all entities. Cash equivalents are held for meeting short-term cash commitments rather than for investment purposes. An investment qualifies as a cash equivalent only when it has a short maturity of, say, three months or less from the date of acquisition (<ParaRef std="IAS 7" para="7" />). Bank overdrafts repayable on demand that form an integral part of an entity's cash management are included as a component of cash and cash equivalents (<ParaRef std="IAS 7" para="8" />).

Key classification issues:
- **Interest and dividends** — Must be disclosed separately and classified consistently period to period. Financial institutions classify interest as operating.
- **Taxes on income** — Classified as operating unless specifically identifiable with financing or investing activities (<ParaRef std="IAS 7" para="35" />).
- **Non-cash transactions** — Excluded from the cash flow statement but disclosed separately (e.g., acquisition of an asset by assuming a directly related liability, conversion of debt to equity).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The cash equivalent boundary is judgment-intensive. <ParaRef std="IAS 7" para="7" /> uses "say, three months" — deliberately imprecise. Equity investments are excluded from cash equivalents (unless they are, in substance, cash equivalents — e.g., preference shares acquired within three months of maturity with a known redemption date). In Nepal, call deposits and money-at-call placements between BFIs are typically classified as cash equivalents; inter-bank lending beyond three months is not.

Scope interactions: (a) restricted cash — regulatory reserves mandated by NRB (cash reserve ratio, statutory liquidity ratio) are not available for general use and should be excluded from cash equivalents, with separate disclosure; (b) foreign currency cash flows — translated at the exchange rate at the date of the cash flow (<ParaRef std="IAS 7" para="25-26" />), but Nepal Rastra Bank's managed exchange rate regime means the NRB mid-rate is typically used; (c) hedging cash flows — operating or financing depending on the hedged item's classification.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

The statement of cash flows reports cash flows during the period classified by operating, investing, and financing activities. Unlike accrual-basis recognition under NAS 1, cash flows are recognised when cash or cash equivalents are received or paid. The indirect method reconciles profit or loss to net cash from operating activities by adjusting for non-cash items (depreciation, impairment losses, provisions), changes in working capital (receivables, payables, inventory), and items whose cash effects relate to investing or financing (gain/loss on asset disposal).

Entities must report separately major classes of gross cash receipts and gross cash payments from investing and financing activities, except where netting is permitted (<ParaRef std="IAS 7" para="22-24" />) — for example, cash receipts and payments on behalf of customers where the cash flows reflect the activities of the customer rather than the entity.

## Measurement

Cash flows are measured at the amounts actually received or paid. Foreign currency cash flows are translated at the exchange rate at the transaction date (<ParaRef std="IAS 7" para="25" />). The effect of exchange rate changes on cash and cash equivalents held in foreign currencies is reported separately to reconcile opening and closing balances. In Nepal, most foreign currency transactions are denominated in USD or INR, and NRB's daily exchange rates are used for translation.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

The statement must show opening and closing balances of cash and cash equivalents, with a reconciliation to the amounts in the statement of financial position. Key disclosures include: the components of cash and cash equivalents and a reconciliation to balance sheet line items; the policy adopted for determining which items are treated as cash equivalents; significant non-cash investing and financing transactions (e.g., conversion of debentures to equity in Nepali companies, acquisition of assets through finance leases); and restricted cash balances with an explanation of restrictions (<ParaRef std="IAS 7" para="48" />).

NRB requires additional disclosure for BFIs: a breakdown of cash flows from lending activities, changes in mandatory reserves, and inter-bank placements. SEBON requires listed entities to publish cash flow statements in quarterly reports alongside the income statement and balance sheet.
