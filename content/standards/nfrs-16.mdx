## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

When a business rents an office, a warehouse, or a vehicle, that is a lease. Before NFRS 16, most rental agreements were treated like a simple monthly expense — just record the rent payment each month. The building never appeared on the company's balance sheet.

NFRS 16 changed this. Now, when a business signs a lease longer than 12 months, it must show two things on its balance sheet: (1) the right to use the rented asset (called a "right-of-use asset"), and (2) the obligation to pay future rent (called a "lease liability").

**Why does this matter?** It gives a more honest picture of what a company owes. Before, a company could rent expensive offices and look debt-free. Now, those future rent payments show up as liabilities.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 16 fundamentally changed lease accounting by eliminating the old operating/finance lease distinction for lessees. Under the previous NAS 17, an operating lease meant off-balance-sheet treatment — a simple rental expense in the income statement. NFRS 16 replaces that with a single on-balance-sheet model: every lease longer than twelve months creates a right-of-use (ROU) asset and a lease liability.

For Nepali entities, this had an outsized impact. Businesses in Kathmandu Valley typically lease office space, retail premises, and warehouse facilities on long-term contracts. Bringing these onto the balance sheet increased total assets and total liabilities materially — in some cases by 15–25% of total assets for service-sector companies.

Lessor accounting, by contrast, remains largely unchanged from NAS 17. Lessors still classify leases as operating or finance and account for them accordingly.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NFRS 16 eliminates the bright-line classification tests of NAS 17 for lessees, implementing a single capitalisation model rooted in the principle that a lease creates a right-of-use asset and a financial liability. The standard's conceptual basis is that a lease is economically equivalent to purchasing the asset with debt financing — the balance sheet should reflect this reality regardless of legal form.

The asymmetric treatment (single model for lessees, dual model retained for lessors) was a pragmatic compromise during the IASB's deliberations. The consequence: sale-and-leaseback transactions require careful analysis at the intersection of NFRS 15 (sale recognition) and NFRS 16 (leaseback measurement), with the seller-lessee recognising only the portion of gain relating to rights transferred (<ParaRef std="IFRS 16" para="100" />).

Nepal's adoption was verbatim. The key implementation challenge was the incremental borrowing rate determination — Nepal lacks a deep corporate bond market, so entities generally bootstrapped rates from bank lending facilities, adjusted for term structure and collateral. ICAN provided no rate-setting guidance, leading to inconsistent practice across sectors.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope & key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NFRS 16 applies to all rental agreements, with two exceptions that make life easier:

- **Short-term leases** (12 months or less) — you can keep treating these as simple rent expense.
- **Low-value items** (roughly under NPR 50,000 when new) — things like office printers or coffee machines. Just expense the payments.

**Key terms:**
- **Lease** — You are paying to use someone else's property or equipment for a set period.
- **Right-of-use asset** — Your right to use the rented item. It goes on your balance sheet like you "own" it during the lease.
- **Lease liability** — The total future rent you owe, calculated at today's value.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The standard applies to all leases, including subleases, with two optional exemptions: **short-term leases** (12 months or less) and **low-value leases** (underlying asset value of roughly USD 5,000 or less when new). Entities electing these exemptions recognise lease payments as an expense on a straight-line basis (<ParaRef std="IFRS 16" para="5" />).

Key terms:

- **Lease** — a contract that conveys the right to control the use of an identified asset for a period of time in exchange for consideration (<ParaRef std="IFRS 16" para="9" />).
- **Right-of-use asset** — represents the lessee's right to use the underlying asset over the lease term.
- **Lease liability** — the present value of future lease payments not yet paid.
- **Lease term** — the non-cancellable period plus any periods covered by extension or termination options the lessee is reasonably certain to exercise.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope determination at <ParaRef std="IFRS 16" para="9" /> hinges on two conditions: (a) an identified asset exists, and (b) the customer has the right to control its use. Control requires both the right to obtain substantially all economic benefits and the right to direct the use of the asset. Supplier substitution rights can negate identification if they are substantive (<ParaRef std="IFRS 16" para="B14" />).

The low-value exemption is assessed on an absolute basis (the value of the asset when new), not relative to the entity's size — a deliberate simplification. The short-term exemption requires reassessment when lease modifications or option reassessments effectively extend the term beyond 12 months.

Lease term determination is a critical judgement: "reasonably certain" is a high threshold, analogous to "virtually certain" in other standards. Economic penalties for non-exercise (including both contractual penalties and the cost of finding alternative assets) must be considered. In Nepal's commercial real estate market, where relocation costs in Kathmandu can be substantial and alternative premises scarce, this assessment often favours including renewal options.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

At commencement, the lessee recognises:

1. A **right-of-use asset** measured at cost — comprising the initial lease liability, any advance payments, initial direct costs, and an estimate of restoration/dismantling costs (<ParaRef std="IFRS 16" para="24" />).
2. A **lease liability** measured at the present value of lease payments, discounted using the interest rate implicit in the lease or, if that rate cannot be readily determined, the lessee's incremental borrowing rate (<ParaRef std="IFRS 16" para="26" />).

In Nepal, the incremental borrowing rate is almost always used because lessors rarely disclose the implicit rate. Entities typically reference their most recent bank lending facility rate, adjusted for the lease term and security.

## Measurement

**Subsequent measurement of the ROU asset:** depreciated on a straight-line basis over the shorter of the lease term and the asset's useful life (unless ownership transfers, in which case over useful life). Impairment testing follows NAS 36 (<ParaRef std="IFRS 16" para="32" />).

**Subsequent measurement of the lease liability:** the carrying amount increases with interest (using the discount rate from initial recognition) and decreases with lease payments made. When payments change due to index adjustments, rate revisions, or reassessed options, the liability is remeasured and the ROU asset adjusted correspondingly (<ParaRef std="IFRS 16" para="36" />).

Variable lease payments tied to an index (e.g., CPI-linked rent escalations) are included in the liability when the index changes. Variable payments based on performance or usage — such as revenue-share rents common in Nepali retail leases — are expensed as incurred.

## Presentation & disclosure

The ROU asset is presented either as a separate line item or within the same line as the underlying asset class (e.g., ROU buildings within property, plant and equipment). The lease liability is presented separately from other financial liabilities or disclosed in the notes (<ParaRef std="IFRS 16" para="47" />).

In the income statement, depreciation of the ROU asset and interest on the lease liability replace the old single operating lease expense. This front-loads total expense recognition compared to straight-line operating lease treatment — a point that catches Nepali preparers off guard when transitioning.

Disclosures include a maturity analysis of lease liabilities, total cash outflows for leases, and qualitative information about extension/termination options and variable payment arrangements.
