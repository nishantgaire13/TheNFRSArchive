## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Insurance is a promise: you pay a premium, and the insurance company pays you if something bad happens — a car accident, a fire, a health emergency, or death. But how does the insurance company account for these promises? That is what NFRS 4 addressed.

NFRS 4 was an **interim standard** — a temporary set of rules that said: "Keep using your existing accounting practices for insurance contracts, but make sure you do a **Liability Adequacy Test (LAT)** to check if your reserves are enough." It was never meant to be permanent. The real, comprehensive standard is **NFRS 17**, which replaced NFRS 4 from **17 July 2024** (1 Shrawan 2081 BS).

In Nepal, insurance is regulated by the **Beema Samiti** (Insurance Board of Nepal). There are about 40 insurance companies — 19 life insurers and 21 non-life (general) insurers. Before NFRS 4, these companies followed Beema Samiti's prescribed formats and the Insurance Act 2049. NFRS 4 allowed them to keep those practices largely intact, with some guardrails.

**Why was NFRS 4 just an interim standard?** Because insurance accounting was so different across countries that the IASB needed time to develop a comprehensive model. That model became IFRS 17 (NFRS 17 in Nepal) — a ground-up rethink of how insurance liabilities are measured and how insurance revenue is recognised.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 4 was adopted in Nepal as part of the broader NFRS convergence effective 16 July 2018. It served as a bridge standard, permitting insurers to continue pre-existing accounting policies for insurance contracts while introducing two key safeguards:

1. **Liability Adequacy Test (LAT)** — At each reporting date, the insurer must assess whether its recognised insurance liabilities are adequate using current estimates of future cash flows. If the test shows a deficiency, the entire deficiency is recognised in profit or loss (<ParaRef std="IFRS 4" para="15–19" />).

2. **Prohibition on catastrophe and equalisation provisions** — NFRS 4 prohibited recognising provisions for possible future claims under contracts not yet in existence ("catastrophe reserves") and provisions to equalise claims across years. These were common under regulatory accounting.

**Scope:** NFRS 4 applied to insurance contracts (including reinsurance contracts) issued by the entity, and reinsurance contracts held. It did not apply to policyholder accounting (the insurance buyer). An **insurance contract** is defined as a contract under which one party (the insurer) accepts **significant insurance risk** from another party (the policyholder) by agreeing to compensate the policyholder if a specified uncertain future event (the insured event) adversely affects the policyholder (<ParaRef std="IFRS 4" para="Appendix A" />).

**Significant insurance risk** is the key — if a contract transfers significant insurance risk, it is within NFRS 4. If not (e.g., investment contracts with trivial insurance risk), it falls under NAS 39/NFRS 9 and NFRS 15.

In Nepal, the distinction affected unit-linked products offered by life insurers. If the insurance element was insignificant relative to the investment element, the contract was unbundled — insurance component under NFRS 4, deposit/investment component under NAS 39/NFRS 9.

**Transition to NFRS 17:** Nepal Insurance Authority (Beema Samiti) mandated NFRS 17 adoption from 1 Shrawan 2081 BS (17 July 2024) for all insurance companies. The transition requires full retrospective application where practicable, with modified retrospective or fair value approaches permitted for groups of contracts where full retrospective is impracticable.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NFRS 4 occupied a unique position in the Nepali standards framework: it explicitly grandfathered existing accounting policies while imposing a floor through the LAT. This meant Nepal's insurers continued using the regulatory framework prescribed by Beema Samiti — which was itself based on Indian IRDA guidelines and the Insurance Act 2049 — with NFRS 4 serving as a thin overlay.

**LAT methodology in Nepal:**
The LAT under NFRS 4 compared recognised insurance liabilities (net of related deferred acquisition costs and intangible assets) against current estimates of future contractual cash flows, including claims handling costs. For life insurers, this meant comparing the actuarial reserve (calculated using the net premium valuation or gross premium valuation method) against a best-estimate liability using current assumptions. Key assumptions: mortality (using Nepal-specific or Indian mortality tables — LIC 2006-08 or IALM 2012-14 were common), morbidity rates, persistency/lapse rates (high in Nepal — 15–25% in first two years for individual policies), and discount rates (Nepal government bond yields).

For non-life insurers, the LAT tested the unexpired risk reserve (URR) against current estimates of future claims and expenses for the unexpired portion of policies in force. Given Nepal's concentrated catastrophic risk exposure (earthquakes, floods), non-life LAT required careful estimation of IBNR (incurred but not reported) claims and catastrophe loading.

**Embedded derivatives in insurance contracts:**
NFRS 4 generally exempted embedded derivatives in insurance contracts from the bifurcation requirements of NAS 39 (<ParaRef std="IFRS 4" para="7–8" />), unless the embedded derivative was itself an insurance contract or the host was a financial instrument rather than an insurance contract. In Nepal, this exemption was relevant for guaranteed annuity options embedded in certain life policies and for minimum guaranteed returns on unit-linked products.

**Reinsurance accounting under NFRS 4:**
Nepal's reinsurers (Nepal Reinsurance Company being the only domestic reinsurer, established 2015) and ceding companies continued existing reinsurance accounting: reinsurance assets were tested for impairment (a cedant's reinsurance receivable from a reinsurer in financial difficulty), and the prohibition on offsetting meant gross and ceded amounts were presented separately. Nepal's mandatory cession requirement (currently 20% to Nepal Re) affected presentation.

**NFRS 17 transition challenges specific to Nepal:**
- Historical data gaps: many insurers lack granular policy-level data needed for building block or premium allocation approaches
- Actuarial capacity: Nepal has fewer than 50 qualified actuaries, and NFRS 17 demands significantly more actuarial input than NFRS 4
- System readiness: NFRS 17 requires sub-annual cohort grouping (annual cohorts, profitability groups) that existing policy administration systems may not support
- Contractual service margin (CSM) calculation: requires transition adjustments to establish CSM at the NFRS 17 effective date for pre-existing contracts
- Discount rate: NFRS 17 requires current discount rates reflecting the characteristics of the cash flows — Nepal's illiquid yield curve makes bottom-up and top-down approaches challenging

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NFRS 4 applied to:
- **Insurance contracts** issued by an insurer (both life and non-life)
- **Reinsurance contracts** (insurance purchased by insurers from other insurers to share risk)

It did **not** apply to:
- Warranties given by manufacturers (covered by NFRS 15)
- Financial guarantee contracts (covered by NAS 39/NFRS 9, unless the issuer elected to apply NFRS 4)
- Insurance contracts where the entity is the policyholder (buyer of insurance)

**Key terms:**
- **Insurance contract** — A contract where the insurer accepts significant insurance risk from the policyholder.
- **Insurance risk** — Risk other than financial risk, transferred from the policyholder to the insurer. Example: the risk that a factory burns down (fire insurance).
- **Liability Adequacy Test (LAT)** — A check at each reporting date to ensure insurance reserves are sufficient to pay future claims.
- **Reinsurance** — Insurance for insurance companies. Nepal Life Insurance may reinsure 50% of a large life policy with Swiss Re.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The **significant insurance risk** test is the gateway to NFRS 4. A contract transfers significant insurance risk if, and only if, an insured event could cause the insurer to pay significant additional benefits in any scenario, excluding scenarios that lack commercial substance. "Significant" is judged on a present value basis and relative to the amounts that would be payable if the insured event did not occur.

Investment contracts with a discretionary participation feature (DPF) — where the policyholder receives supplementary benefits based on fund performance at the issuer's discretion — could be accounted for under NFRS 4 even if the insurance risk was not significant, provided the issuer elected to do so. In Nepal, certain endowment and money-back policies with guaranteed maturity values plus discretionary bonuses contained DPF elements.

**Unbundling:** NFRS 4 required unbundling of deposit components from insurance contracts when the deposit component could be measured separately and the insurer's accounting policies did not otherwise recognise all obligations and rights arising from the deposit component. This was relevant for unit-linked products where the investment account value was distinct from the mortality/morbidity cover.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The boundary between insurance contracts (NFRS 4) and financial instruments (NAS 39/NFRS 9) determined which measurement framework applied — a classification with significant P&L implications. In Nepal, Beema Samiti's product approval process did not historically consider NFRS classification, meaning some approved products straddled the boundary. Micro-insurance products, increasingly important in Nepal's financial inclusion agenda, generally transfer significant insurance risk (crop insurance, livestock insurance) and fell squarely within NFRS 4.

The DPF election was strategically important: by electing NFRS 4 treatment for DPF investment contracts, life insurers avoided the complexities of NAS 39/NFRS 9 financial liability measurement (particularly the fair value option and own credit risk adjustments) for products that were economically part of their insurance operations.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

Under NFRS 4, insurers continued their existing recognition policies for premiums, claims, and insurance liabilities, subject to the LAT floor and the prohibition on catastrophe/equalisation provisions. There was no prescribed recognition model — the standard explicitly permitted continuation of local GAAP or regulatory practices.

In Nepal, this meant:
- **Premium recognition:** Life premiums recognised when due from policyholders. Non-life premiums recognised at inception of risk, with unearned premium reserve (UPR) for unexpired risk.
- **Claims recognition:** Claims recognised when the insured event occurs and the obligation to pay is established. IBNR (incurred but not reported) provisions required actuarial estimation.
- **Acquisition costs:** Some insurers deferred acquisition costs (commissions, underwriting costs), others expensed immediately. NFRS 4 permitted either approach.

## Measurement

**Liability Adequacy Test (LAT):**
At each reporting date, the insurer must assess whether its recognised insurance liabilities are adequate. The test uses **current estimates** of future cash flows under insurance contracts. If the assessment shows the carrying amount of liabilities (less related DAC and intangible assets) is inadequate, the entire deficiency is recognised in P&L immediately.

In Nepal's non-life sector, the LAT typically compared the unearned premium reserve against expected future claims and expenses for the unexpired risk period. For life insurers, the LAT compared actuarial reserves against current best-estimate liabilities using updated assumptions for mortality, persistency, and investment returns.

**Minimum LAT requirements** (<ParaRef std="IFRS 4" para="16" />): the test must consider current estimates of all contractual cash flows and related cash flows (claims handling costs). If existing policies already required a test meeting these minimum requirements, NFRS 4 imposed no additional obligation.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

NFRS 4 required insurers to disclose:
- Accounting policies for insurance contracts and related assets, liabilities, income, and expense.
- The recognised assets, liabilities, income, and expense arising from insurance contracts.
- The process used to determine assumptions with the greatest effect on measurement, including quantitative sensitivity analysis.
- Reconciliation of changes in insurance liabilities, reinsurance assets, and related DAC.
- Information about insurance risk: sensitivity to insurance risk, concentrations of insurance risk, and actual claims compared to previous estimates (claims development tables for non-life, though NFRS 4 encouraged rather than required these).

Beema Samiti additionally required prescribed formats for financial statements, solvency margin calculations, and segregated fund disclosures. These regulatory requirements operated alongside — and in some cases exceeded — NFRS 4's disclosure requirements.
