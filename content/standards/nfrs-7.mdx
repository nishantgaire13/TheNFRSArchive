## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Financial instruments — loans, deposits, shares, bonds, and derivatives — are at the heart of banking and many other businesses. But simply showing these items on the balance sheet is not enough. Users of financial statements need to understand the **risks** associated with these instruments. NFRS 7 requires companies to disclose detailed information about how significant financial instruments are to the business and what risks they face.

Think of it like a health check-up for a bank. The balance sheet tells you the bank has NPR 50 arba in loans, but NFRS 7 disclosures tell you: How many of those loans might not be repaid (credit risk)? Does the bank have enough cash to pay depositors who want to withdraw (liquidity risk)? What happens to the bank's earnings if interest rates change by 1% (market risk)? These are the questions NFRS 7 forces entities to answer.

In Nepal, BFIs such as Nabil Bank, Himalayan Bank, and Nepal Life Insurance produce the most extensive NFRS 7 disclosures because their balance sheets are dominated by financial instruments. But even trading companies and manufacturers must disclose information about their borrowings, receivables, and any forward contracts or hedging arrangements.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 7 establishes two broad categories of disclosure: (1) the **significance of financial instruments** for the entity's financial position and performance, and (2) the **nature and extent of risks** arising from financial instruments — specifically credit risk, liquidity risk, and market risk (which includes interest rate risk, currency risk, and other price risk).

For significance disclosures, the standard requires information about the carrying amounts of each category of financial asset and liability (FVTPL, amortised cost, FVOCI), details of reclassifications between categories, information about financial assets pledged as collateral, and the allowance account for credit losses. For risk disclosures, NFRS 7 requires both qualitative information (risk management policies and processes) and quantitative data (concentrations of risk, sensitivity analyses, maturity analyses).

In Nepal, NRB mandates additional risk disclosures for BFIs beyond what NFRS 7 requires — including sector-wise credit concentration (real estate, hydropower, agriculture), geography-wise credit distribution, single obligor limits, and capital adequacy ratios. These NRB requirements complement and sometimes exceed NFRS 7, making BFI financial statements among the most disclosure-heavy in Nepal. SEBON-listed non-financial entities must also comply with NFRS 7 for their financial instruments, though the disclosures are typically less extensive because financial instruments are a smaller proportion of their balance sheets.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NFRS 7 operates as a pure disclosure standard — it does not affect recognition or measurement, which remain governed by NFRS 9 and NAS 32. However, its interaction with NFRS 9's expected credit loss (ECL) model has dramatically expanded disclosure requirements since Nepal's NFRS adoption. The credit risk disclosures under <ParaRef std="IFRS 7" para="35A-35N" /> require entities to explain their ECL methodologies, including how they define "significant increase in credit risk" (Stage 2 migration), how they incorporate forward-looking information, and how they determine whether a financial asset is credit-impaired (Stage 3).

The risk disclosures require sensitivity analyses that go beyond simple repricing gap analysis. For interest rate risk, entities must disclose the effect on profit or loss and equity of reasonably possible changes in interest rates (<ParaRef std="IFRS 7" para="40-42" />). For Nepal's BFIs, this requires modelling the impact of NRB base rate corridor changes on net interest income — a complex exercise given the mix of fixed and floating rate loans, rate reset frequencies, and the behavioural characteristics of demand deposits (which contractually reprice immediately but practically exhibit stability).

Nepal-specific challenges include: (a) credit risk concentration disclosures reveal systemic vulnerabilities — many BFIs have 15-25% real estate exposure despite NRB sector limits, requiring careful disclosure of how limits are monitored and breaches addressed; (b) liquidity risk disclosures must reflect the unique characteristics of Nepal's financial system, including limited interbank market depth, cash reserve ratio requirements, and the seasonal liquidity cycle (tight during Dashain-Tihar, flush during remittance peaks); (c) fair value hierarchy disclosures (<ParaRef std="IFRS 7" para="25-30" />) are challenging because Nepal lacks deep secondary markets — most financial instruments are Level 2 or Level 3, requiring disclosure of valuation techniques and unobservable inputs.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NFRS 7 applies to **all entities** that hold financial instruments — not just banks. A financial instrument is any contract that gives rise to a financial asset of one entity and a financial liability or equity instrument of another. Even a simple trade receivable or bank borrowing is a financial instrument.

**Key terms:**
- **Credit risk** — The risk that the other party will not pay what it owes. For a bank, it is the risk that borrowers will not repay their loans.
- **Liquidity risk** — The risk that the entity will have difficulty meeting its financial obligations when they fall due.
- **Market risk** — The risk that fair values or cash flows will change because of market price movements. Includes interest rate risk, currency risk, and equity price risk.
- **Sensitivity analysis** — A calculation showing how much profit or equity would change if a risk factor (e.g., interest rates) moved by a specified amount.
- **Fair value hierarchy** — A three-level classification of how fair value is determined: Level 1 (market prices), Level 2 (observable inputs), Level 3 (unobservable inputs).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 7 applies to all recognised and unrecognised financial instruments (<ParaRef std="IFRS 7" para="4" />), with limited exemptions for interests in subsidiaries, associates, and joint ventures accounted for under NFRS 10, NAS 28, or NFRS 11 (unless those interests are accounted for as financial instruments under NFRS 9). Loan commitments and financial guarantee contracts outside NFRS 9's scope are partially exempt.

Key technical concepts:
- **Classes of financial instruments** — Entities must group instruments into classes appropriate to the nature of information disclosed and the characteristics of the instruments (<ParaRef std="IFRS 7" para="6" />). Classes are more granular than NFRS 9 measurement categories — e.g., "corporate loans," "retail mortgages," and "microfinance loans" might be separate classes within the amortised cost category.
- **Concentration of risk** — Disclosures about concentrations arising from financial instruments sharing similar characteristics (industry, geography, counterparty type) and being affected similarly by changes in economic conditions (<ParaRef std="IFRS 7" para="34(c)" />).
- **Transferred financial assets** — Disclosures about financial assets that have been transferred but not derecognised, or derecognised but with continuing involvement — relevant for BFIs engaged in securitisation or repo transactions.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope interaction between NFRS 7 and NAS 32 is important: NAS 32 addresses presentation (classification of instruments as debt or equity, offsetting criteria), while NFRS 7 addresses disclosure. Both standards apply simultaneously. For Nepal's BFIs, this means that instruments like perpetual bonds (potentially qualifying as Tier 1 capital under NRB norms) must be classified per NAS 32 and disclosed per NFRS 7, with reconciliation to NRB's regulatory capital framework.

The "own use" exemption (<ParaRef std="IFRS 7" para="5" />) excludes contracts to buy or sell a non-financial item for the entity's own use — e.g., a forward contract by Surya Nepal to purchase tobacco at a fixed price for its own manufacturing. However, if such contracts can be net settled, they may fall within scope unless the own-use exemption is elected. In Nepal, own-use contracts are common for import-dependent manufacturers hedging foreign currency exposure through forward contracts with BFIs.

The interaction with NRB's regulatory disclosure requirements creates a dual disclosure regime for BFIs. NRB Unified Directives require sector-wise loan concentration tables, single obligor limit compliance, capital adequacy computations, and credit-to-deposit ratios — some of which overlap with NFRS 7 but use different definitions and thresholds. Practitioners must ensure that NFRS 7 disclosures in NFRS-compliant financial statements are not confused with or replaced by NRB regulatory disclosures, even when both appear in the same annual report.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

NFRS 7 is a disclosure standard and does not establish recognition criteria. The recognition and derecognition of financial instruments are governed by NFRS 9. However, NFRS 7 requires disclosures about the recognition and derecognition policies applied — including the criteria for initial recognition, the basis for derecognition of financial assets (pass-through and continuing involvement assessments), and the treatment of day-one gains or losses when fair value at initial recognition differs from transaction price.

For Nepal's BFIs, the most important recognition-related disclosures concern the expected credit loss (ECL) model under NFRS 9. NFRS 7 requires disclosure of how the ECL model is applied, including the definition of default, the basis for staging (12-month ECL for Stage 1, lifetime ECL for Stages 2 and 3), and how forward-looking macroeconomic scenarios are incorporated. BFIs must reconcile their NFRS 9 ECL provisions with NRB's regulatory provisioning requirements, which often produce different results.

## Measurement

NFRS 7 does not prescribe measurement — that is the domain of NFRS 9. However, the standard requires extensive disclosures about measurement, particularly fair value measurement. Entities must disclose the fair value of each class of financial asset and liability, the methods and significant assumptions used, and the level within the fair value hierarchy (Level 1, 2, or 3). For Level 3 fair values, entities must provide reconciliations from opening to closing balances, disclose the valuation techniques and inputs used, and provide sensitivity analysis to reasonably possible alternative assumptions.

In Nepal, most financial instruments held by BFIs are measured at amortised cost (loans and deposits) or FVOCI (government bonds, quoted equity investments). Fair value measurement challenges arise for: unquoted equity instruments (investments in unlisted companies, often valued using net asset approaches), complex loan restructurings (where modified cash flows require present value calculations using original effective interest rates), and derivatives (interest rate swaps or currency forwards, where counterparty credit risk adjustments are difficult to calibrate in Nepal's shallow derivatives market).

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

NFRS 7 is primarily a disclosure standard, and its requirements are extensive. For the statement of financial position, entities must disclose carrying amounts by measurement category (FVTPL, FVOCI, amortised cost), information about collateral (financial assets pledged, collateral held that can be sold or repledged), and the allowance for credit losses with a reconciliation of changes. For profit or loss, entities must disclose net gains or losses on each category, interest income and expense, fee income, and impairment losses.

The risk disclosures form the most voluminous part of NFRS 7 compliance. Credit risk disclosures include: maximum exposure, credit quality analysis by risk grade, aging analysis of past-due assets, and information about collateral and credit enhancements. Liquidity risk disclosures include a maturity analysis of financial liabilities showing remaining contractual maturities. Market risk disclosures include sensitivity analysis for each type of market risk. In Nepal, NRB requires BFIs to publish a comprehensive risk management report alongside financial statements — entities must ensure consistency between NFRS 7 disclosures in the financial statements and the NRB-mandated risk management disclosures. SEBON additionally requires listed entities to disclose the impact of financial instrument risks on the entity's ability to continue as a going concern.
