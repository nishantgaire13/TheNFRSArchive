## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Imagine you run a cement factory and one of your trucks damages a neighbour's wall. You know you will have to pay for repairs, but the exact amount is uncertain. NAS 37 tells you when and how to record such uncertain obligations in your financial statements.

A **provision** is a liability where the timing or amount is uncertain — like setting aside money for a future bill you know is coming but cannot pin down exactly. A **contingent liability** is a possible obligation that depends on something uncertain happening — like a court case you might lose. A **contingent asset** is a possible benefit — like a court case you might win.

The basic rule: if it is probable you will have to pay and you can estimate the amount, record a provision. If it is only possible, disclose it as a contingent liability. If it is remote, do nothing. For example, if Pashupati Cement faces a lawsuit and their lawyers say they will "probably" lose, a provision is required. If the outcome is uncertain, they disclose it in the notes.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 37 establishes a framework for recognising and measuring provisions — liabilities of uncertain timing or amount — and for disclosing contingent liabilities and contingent assets. The standard prevents entities from either understating liabilities (by ignoring probable obligations) or overstating them (by creating hidden reserves through excessive provisioning).

In Nepal's context, NAS 37 is critical for BFIs managing loan loss provisions (though NFRS 9 ECL now governs financial instrument impairment), hydropower companies with decommissioning obligations, manufacturing firms with warranty liabilities, and any entity facing litigation in Nepal's notoriously slow court system where cases can remain pending for over a decade. Tax disputes with IRD are another common source of provisions.

NAS 37 is fully aligned with IAS 37. The standard draws a clear line between provisions (recognised on the balance sheet) and contingent liabilities (disclosed in notes only).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 37 operationalises the liability recognition criteria from the Conceptual Framework by establishing a three-part test: present obligation from a past event, probable outflow of economic benefits, and reliable estimate of the amount (<ParaRef std="IAS 37" para="14" />). The standard's interaction with NFRS 9 is critical — loan loss provisions for BFIs are now governed by NFRS 9's expected credit loss model, not NAS 37's incurred-loss approach, yet NAS 37 remains relevant for non-financial obligations within financial institutions.

Nepal's legal environment creates unique challenges. Court cases pending 10-15 years make probability assessment extraordinarily difficult — the "probable" threshold under NAS 37 requires more than 50% likelihood, but how does one assess probability when the judicial timeline itself is uncertain? The interaction between NAS 37 and NAS 12 is also significant: provisions that are not tax-deductible until paid create deductible temporary differences requiring deferred tax asset recognition.

Restructuring provisions (<ParaRef std="IAS 37" para="70-83" />) require a detailed formal plan and a valid expectation in those affected — a constructive obligation. In Nepal, where labour laws make retrenchment complex and employee unions are active, the point at which a restructuring obligation becomes "constructive" requires careful judgment.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 37 applies to all provisions, contingent liabilities, and contingent assets except those covered by other standards (like employee benefits under NAS 19, or income taxes under NAS 12).

**Key terms:**
- **Provision** — A liability where you know roughly what you owe but not the exact amount or when.
- **Contingent liability** — A possible obligation that depends on an uncertain future event (like a court verdict), or a present obligation you cannot reliably measure. Not recorded — only disclosed.
- **Contingent asset** — A possible benefit depending on an uncertain event (like winning a lawsuit). Never recorded — disclosed only when probable.
- **Onerous contract** — A contract where the unavoidable costs of meeting the obligations exceed the expected benefits.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The standard applies to all entities for provisions, contingent liabilities, and contingent assets, except where another standard deals with specific types (<ParaRef std="IAS 37" para="1-5" />). Key exclusions: financial instruments (NFRS 9), executory contracts (unless onerous), insurance contracts (NFRS 17), income taxes (NAS 12), employee benefits (NAS 19), and leases (NFRS 16).

- **Present obligation** — legal (from contract, legislation, or law) or constructive (from established pattern of practice creating valid expectations in third parties).
- **Obligating event** — past event that creates an obligation the entity has no realistic alternative to settling.
- **Best estimate** — amount an entity would rationally pay to settle or transfer the obligation at the reporting date (<ParaRef std="IAS 37" para="36" />).
- **Expected value** — probability-weighted average of possible outcomes, appropriate for large populations of items (e.g., warranty claims).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope boundary between NAS 37 and NFRS 9 is a frequent source of confusion. BFI loan loss provisions fall under NFRS 9 (ECL model), but guarantees given (unless financial guarantee contracts under NFRS 9) and legal claims against the bank remain under NAS 37. Similarly, the interaction with NFRS 16 means that onerous lease provisions are now handled through NFRS 16's right-of-use asset impairment, not NAS 37 (<ParaRef std="IAS 37" para="5" />).

Constructive obligations are particularly relevant in Nepal where informal business practices create expectations. A hydropower company that has historically compensated downstream communities for water flow changes may have a constructive obligation even without a legal requirement. The "no realistic alternative to settling" test (<ParaRef std="IAS 37" para="17" />) must consider reputational and commercial consequences, not just legal enforceability.

The distinction between provisions and contingent liabilities hinges on probability — "probable" means more likely than not (>50%). In practice, Nepali entities often struggle with this threshold for litigation provisions, particularly when cases involve government bodies like IRD where settlement patterns are unpredictable.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

A provision is recognised when three conditions are met simultaneously: (1) the entity has a present obligation (legal or constructive) as a result of a past event, (2) it is probable that an outflow of economic benefits will be required to settle the obligation, and (3) a reliable estimate can be made of the amount (<ParaRef std="IAS 37" para="14" />).

Contingent liabilities are not recognised but disclosed unless the possibility of outflow is remote. Contingent assets are never recognised — they are disclosed only when an inflow is probable. If an inflow is virtually certain, it is no longer contingent and is recognised as an asset.

## Measurement

The amount recognised as a provision is the **best estimate** of the expenditure required to settle the obligation at the reporting date (<ParaRef std="IAS 37" para="36" />). For a large population of items (e.g., warranty claims), the expected value method (probability-weighted average) is used. For a single obligation (e.g., a lawsuit), the most likely outcome may be the best estimate, but other possible outcomes must still be considered.

Where the effect of the time value of money is material, the provision is discounted to present value using a pre-tax rate reflecting current market assessments (<ParaRef std="IAS 37" para="45" />). This is particularly relevant for decommissioning obligations on hydropower plants where the outflow may be 25-30 years away. Provisions are reviewed at each reporting date and adjusted to reflect the current best estimate.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

Provisions must be presented separately from trade payables and other liabilities on the balance sheet. For each class of provision, entities must disclose: carrying amount at beginning and end of period, additional provisions made, amounts used, amounts reversed, and the increase during the period due to unwinding of discount (<ParaRef std="IAS 37" para="84" />).

For contingent liabilities, entities disclose a brief description of the nature, an estimate of financial effect where practicable, an indication of uncertainties relating to the amount or timing, and the possibility of any reimbursement. In extremely rare cases where disclosure would seriously prejudice the entity's position in a dispute, the standard permits non-disclosure of specific details — but a general description of the dispute must still be given (<ParaRef std="IAS 37" para="92" />).
