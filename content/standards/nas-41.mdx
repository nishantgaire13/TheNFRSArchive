## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Nepal's economy depends heavily on agriculture — from tea gardens in Ilam to sugarcane farms in the Terai, from poultry farms to fish ponds. NAS 41 tells businesses how to account for **living things** (biological assets) and the products they harvest.

A **biological asset** is a living animal or plant managed by a business — dairy cows, tea bushes, fish, chickens, or timber plantations. **Agricultural produce** is what you harvest from those assets — milk from cows, tea leaves from bushes, eggs from chickens, or logs from trees.

The key rule is that biological assets and harvested produce should be measured at **fair value less costs to sell**. For example, if a poultry farm in Chitwan has 10,000 chickens worth NPR 500 each (market price minus selling costs), they are recorded at NPR 50 lakh — regardless of what it cost to raise them. After harvest, the produce (eggs, meat) leaves NAS 41 and enters NAS 2 (Inventories) for further processing.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 41 prescribes the accounting treatment for biological assets (living animals and plants), agricultural produce at the point of harvest, and government grants related to biological assets. The standard's core principle is fair value measurement — biological assets are measured at fair value less costs to sell at each reporting date, with changes in fair value recognised in profit or loss.

This standard is highly relevant to Nepal, where agriculture contributes approximately 25% of GDP. Key sectors include tea estates in Ilam and Jhapa, sugarcane farming in the Terai, livestock and poultry operations, fish farming, and forestry. However, the fair value measurement requirement creates practical challenges — many of Nepal's agricultural markets are informal, price discovery is limited, and subsistence farming dominates rather than commercial operations with active market participation.

A major 2016 amendment moved **bearer plants** (tea bushes, rubber trees, fruit trees) from NAS 41 to NAS 16 (PPE). The biological produce growing on those plants (tea leaves, rubber latex, fruit) remains under NAS 41 until the point of harvest. NAS 41 is fully aligned with IAS 41.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 41's fair value model creates a fundamental tension with Nepal's agricultural reality. The standard presumes the existence of active markets for biological assets and agricultural produce — but in Nepal, many biological assets (particularly livestock and forestry) trade in informal local markets where prices are negotiated bilaterally, transaction volumes are unreported, and quality grading is absent. The fair value hierarchy under NFRS 13 becomes critical: Level 1 inputs (quoted prices in active markets) are available for commodities like tea at auction, but most biological assets require Level 2 or Level 3 measurement.

The bearer plant amendment (<ParaRef std="IAS 41" para="2" />) fundamentally changed tea estate accounting. Pre-amendment, tea bushes were biological assets measured at fair value. Post-amendment, they are PPE under NAS 16, measured at cost less depreciation (or revaluation). Only the growing tea leaves on the bushes remain biological assets under NAS 41 — measured at fair value less costs to sell until plucked. This bifurcation requires careful cost allocation between the bearer plant (NAS 16) and the produce (NAS 41).

Government grants for biological assets follow a simplified model under NAS 41 rather than the NAS 20 framework. An unconditional grant is recognised in P&L when receivable; a conditional grant is recognised when conditions are met (<ParaRef std="IAS 41" para="34-38" />). Nepal's agricultural subsidies (fertiliser subsidies, livestock development grants) interact with this provision.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 41 covers all **biological assets** (living animals and plants) managed by a business for agricultural purposes, and the **agricultural produce** at the moment of harvest. After harvest, the produce is treated as inventory under NAS 2.

**Key terms:**
- **Biological asset** — A living animal or plant. Examples: dairy cows, tea bushes, fish, timber trees, poultry.
- **Agricultural produce** — The harvested product from a biological asset. Examples: milk, plucked tea leaves, harvested fish, felled logs, eggs.
- **Harvest** — The moment produce is detached from the biological asset or the biological asset's life processes cease.
- **Bearer plant** — A plant used to produce agricultural produce over multiple periods (like tea bushes or fruit trees). These are now under NAS 16, not NAS 41.
- **Biological transformation** — Growth, degeneration, production, and procreation that change a biological asset.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 41 applies to biological assets (except bearer plants), agricultural produce at the point of harvest, and government grants related to biological assets (<ParaRef std="IAS 41" para="1" />). After harvest, agricultural produce falls under NAS 2 or another applicable standard.

Exclusions: land related to agricultural activity (NAS 16 or NAS 40), bearer plants (NAS 16 since 2016 amendment), intangible assets related to agriculture (NAS 38), and right-of-use assets for leased agricultural land (NFRS 16).

- **Agricultural activity** — management of biological transformation and harvest for sale, or conversion into agricultural produce or additional biological assets (<ParaRef std="IAS 41" para="5" />).
- **Costs to sell** — incremental costs directly attributable to disposal, excluding finance costs and income taxes. Include broker commissions, market levies, and transport to market.
- **Group of biological assets** — aggregation of similar living animals or plants (e.g., a herd of cattle, a plantation of tea bushes as bearer plants vs. the leaves on them).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope boundary for agricultural activity requires management of biological transformation — mere harvesting from unmanaged sources (wild fishing, deforestation of natural forests) is excluded (<ParaRef std="IAS 41" para="6" />). This is relevant in Nepal's community forestry context, where user groups manage forest resources — the management element may bring community forestry within NAS 41's scope if the entity exercises control over the biological assets.

The produce vs. processing distinction determines the NAS 41 boundary. Tea leaves at the point of plucking are agricultural produce (NAS 41); processed tea is inventory (NAS 2). Sugarcane at harvest is NAS 41; sugar is NAS 2. The standard applies only up to harvest point — all subsequent processing, storage, and sale follow other standards.

Fair value measurement for biological assets in Nepal requires careful navigation of NFRS 13's hierarchy. For livestock, local haat bazaar (periodic markets) may provide observable prices, but these are often not "active markets" in the NFRS 13 sense. Entities may need to use income approaches (present value of expected net cash flows from the asset) when market-based evidence is insufficient — particularly for forestry assets with long rotation cycles (15-25 years for sal or teak plantations in the Terai).

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

A biological asset or agricultural produce is recognised when the entity controls the asset as a result of past events, it is probable that future economic benefits will flow to the entity, and the fair value or cost can be measured reliably (<ParaRef std="IAS 41" para="10" />). Control is typically evidenced by legal ownership (e.g., branded livestock) but may also arise from other means (e.g., cattle obtained at birth).

Agricultural produce is recognised at the point of harvest at fair value less costs to sell. This amount becomes the cost basis for subsequent accounting under NAS 2 (Inventories) or another applicable standard.

## Measurement

Biological assets are measured at **fair value less costs to sell** at initial recognition and at each subsequent reporting date, with changes recognised in profit or loss (<ParaRef std="IAS 41" para="12-13" />). The presumption is that fair value can be measured reliably. Only if fair value cannot be measured reliably on initial recognition (and market-determined prices are not available and alternative estimates would be clearly unreliable) is the cost model permitted — and only until fair value becomes reliably measurable.

Agricultural produce at harvest is always measured at fair value less costs to sell. The fair value at harvest becomes the deemed cost when the produce enters inventory under NAS 2. For example, tea leaves plucked from the bush are measured at fair value less costs to sell at harvest — this amount becomes the cost input for the tea processing under NAS 2.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

Entities must present the carrying amount of biological assets separately on the balance sheet. The statement of profit or loss must show the aggregate gain or loss from initial recognition of biological assets and agricultural produce, and from changes in fair value less costs to sell (<ParaRef std="IAS 41" para="40" />).

Key disclosures include: a description of each group of biological assets (narrative and quantified), the methods and significant assumptions used in determining fair value, the fair value less costs to sell of agricultural produce harvested during the period, existence and carrying amounts of biological assets with restricted title or pledged as security, commitments for development or acquisition of biological assets, and risk management strategies related to agricultural activity.
