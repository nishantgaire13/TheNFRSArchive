## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Sometimes the government gives money, land, or other help to businesses. These are called "government grants." For example, the Nepal government might give NPR 50 lakh to a farmer to buy modern irrigation equipment, or provide subsidised interest rates to a small business through Nepal Rastra Bank's refinance facility.

The key question NAS 20 answers is: **when and how do you record this help in your accounts?** You don't just record it as income the moment you receive it. Instead, you match the grant income to the costs it is meant to cover. If the government gives you NPR 1 crore to build a factory that will last 20 years, you spread the grant income over those 20 years — not all in year one.

**Why does this matter?** Without this standard, a company receiving a large grant would show a massive profit in one year and normal profits in later years. NAS 20 ensures profits are reported smoothly and honestly.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 20 establishes the accounting treatment for government grants — transfers of resources from government to an entity in return for compliance with certain conditions related to the entity's operating activities. It also addresses disclosure of other forms of government assistance that do not meet the strict grant definition.

Nepal's economic policy relies heavily on government grants and subsidies: agricultural subsidies through the Department of Agriculture, interest subsidies via NRB's refinance facility, hydropower development incentives, and COVID-era concessional loan programmes. The standard requires grants to be recognised in profit or loss on a systematic basis over the periods in which the entity recognises the related costs. Grants related to assets are presented either as deferred income or as a deduction from the carrying amount of the asset.

NAS 20 is fully aligned with IAS 20 — no carve-outs exist in Nepal's adoption.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 20 applies an income approach rather than a capital approach: government grants are recognised in profit or loss over the periods necessary to match them with the related costs they are intended to compensate (<ParaRef std="IAS 20" para="12" />). The standard prohibits crediting grants directly to equity — a principle that interacts with the Conceptual Framework's distinction between performance and capital contributions.

The "reasonable assurance" recognition threshold (<ParaRef std="IAS 20" para="7" />) requires both (a) assurance that the entity will comply with conditions and (b) assurance that the grant will be received. In Nepal, this creates practical challenges: government budget releases are often delayed across fiscal years (Shrawan to Ashad), provincial grants may be approved but unfunded, and conditional grants from NRB's refinance facility require ongoing compliance verification. The standard's silence on probability thresholds means judgement is required, particularly for multi-year conditional grants common in Nepal's hydropower and agricultural sectors.

Non-monetary grants (free land, concessional-rate loans) require careful measurement. NRB's concessional lending programmes create below-market-rate loan benefits that must be measured under NFRS 9's fair value requirements, with the grant element recognised under NAS 20.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope & key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 20 covers any benefit a business receives from the government, including:
- **Cash grants** — direct money from the government (e.g., NPR 20 lakh subsidy for a tea processing unit)
- **Asset grants** — free or cheap land, equipment, or buildings
- **Interest subsidies** — the government pays part of your loan interest
- **Tax holidays** — not covered by NAS 20 (these fall under NAS 12 Income Taxes)

**Key terms:**
- **Government grant** — help from the government in the form of transfers of resources, in return for meeting certain conditions.
- **Grants related to assets** — grants whose main condition is that you buy or build a long-term asset.
- **Grants related to income** — grants meant to cover your day-to-day expenses.
- **Deferred income** — grant money received but not yet recognised as income because you haven't yet incurred the related costs.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The standard applies to government grants as defined in <ParaRef std="IAS 20" para="3" /> — transfers of resources to an entity in return for past or future compliance with conditions relating to operating activities. Government assistance that cannot reasonably have a value placed on it (e.g., free technical advice) and government transactions in the normal course of trade are excluded.

Key definitions:
- **Government** — includes government agencies and similar bodies, whether local, national, or international.
- **Government assistance** — action by government designed to provide an economic benefit specific to an entity or range of entities qualifying under certain criteria.
- **Forgivable loans** — loans that the lender undertakes to waive repayment under certain prescribed conditions — treated as grants when there is reasonable assurance that conditions will be met.
- **Below-market-rate loans** — the benefit of a government loan at below-market interest is treated as a government grant, measured as the difference between proceeds and fair value of the loan under NFRS 9.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope boundary with NFRS 15 is critical: government grants within NAS 20 involve non-exchange transfers (the government does not receive equivalent value in return). Where a government entity purchases goods or services at market rates, NFRS 15 applies. Nepal's procurement framework — where government agencies purchase infrastructure services — often blurs this line, requiring analysis of whether the transaction is a grant with conditions or a commercial contract.

NAS 20 does not address government grants covered by NAS 41 (Agriculture) — biological asset grants are within NAS 41's fair-value-less-costs-to-sell framework. The interaction with NFRS 9 for below-market loans is procedurally complex: the loan is initially recognised at fair value (discounted cash flows at a market rate), with the difference between nominal and fair value recognised as a government grant under NAS 20. Forgivable loans (<ParaRef std="IAS 20" para="10A" />) are treated as grants only when there is reasonable assurance that forgiveness conditions will be met — otherwise, they remain financial liabilities under NFRS 9.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

Government grants are recognised when there is reasonable assurance that (a) the entity will comply with the conditions attached and (b) the grant will be received (<ParaRef std="IAS 20" para="7" />). Receipt of a grant does not of itself provide conclusive evidence that conditions have been or will be met. The manner of receipt — cash, asset reduction, or forgiveness of liability — does not affect the accounting method. Grants are recognised in profit or loss on a systematic basis over the periods in which the entity recognises the costs they are intended to compensate (<ParaRef std="IAS 20" para="12" />).

## Measurement

Grants related to assets are presented either by setting up the grant as deferred income (recognised over the asset's useful life) or by deducting the grant from the asset's carrying amount (reducing future depreciation). Grants related to income are presented either as a credit in profit or loss (separately or under "other income") or as a deduction from the related expense (<ParaRef std="IAS 20" para="29" />). Non-monetary grants may be recorded at fair value or at a nominal amount. Repayment of a government grant is accounted for as a change in accounting estimate under NAS 8 — for asset-related grants, the cumulative additional depreciation that would have been charged is recognised immediately in profit or loss (<ParaRef std="IAS 20" para="32" />).

## Presentation & disclosure

The accounting policy adopted for grants, the nature and extent of grants recognised, and any unfulfilled conditions and contingencies must be disclosed (<ParaRef std="IAS 20" para="39" />). Grants related to assets are presented on the balance sheet either as deferred income or as a deduction from the asset. Other forms of government assistance — where the value cannot be reasonably measured — must still be disclosed with sufficient information for users to understand the nature, extent, and duration of the assistance received.
