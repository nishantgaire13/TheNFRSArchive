## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

When a company searches for minerals underground — whether it is limestone for cement, iron ore, or even oil and gas — it spends a lot of money before it knows whether there is anything worth extracting. These costs might include geological surveys, drilling test holes, analysing soil samples, and obtaining exploration licenses. NFRS 6 provides guidance on how companies account for these exploration and evaluation (E&E) costs.

The big question is simple: should these costs be treated as an expense (reducing profit immediately) or as an asset (recorded on the balance sheet to be matched against future revenue when the minerals are actually extracted)? NFRS 6 allows companies significant flexibility — they can develop their own accounting policy for E&E expenditures, as long as it is applied consistently and meets certain quality criteria.

In Nepal, the mining sector is limited but growing. The most significant activities involve limestone quarrying for cement production (companies like Hetauda Cement, Udayapur Cement), dimension stone quarrying, and small-scale mining operations. Nepal also has some hydrocarbon exploration potential in the Terai region, though this remains largely untapped. For most Nepali entities, NFRS 6 has limited practical application, but it becomes relevant when companies invest in exploration activities — even at a small scale compared to global mining operations.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 6 provides a temporary exemption from certain requirements of the Conceptual Framework and other NFRS during the exploration and evaluation phase of mineral resource extraction. The standard permits entities to develop their own accounting policies for E&E expenditures and to retain those policies from pre-NFRS adoption, provided they result in information that is relevant and reliable. This flexibility was granted because the IASB recognised that developing comprehensive guidance for extractive industries required a longer-term project.

The standard covers expenditures incurred after the entity has obtained legal rights to explore a specific area and before the technical feasibility and commercial viability of extracting a mineral resource are demonstrable. Once technical feasibility and commercial viability are established, the expenditures are no longer within NFRS 6's scope — they transition to development-phase accounting under other standards (NAS 16 for tangible assets, NAS 38 for intangible assets).

In Nepal, the Department of Mines and Geology issues exploration licenses for various minerals. Entities with such licenses — primarily cement manufacturers exploring limestone deposits, marble quarry operators, and companies involved in sand and aggregate extraction — must determine their accounting policy for E&E expenditures. The choice between capitalisation and immediate expensing has a significant impact on reported profits, particularly for companies in the early stages of exploration.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NFRS 6 represents a rare concession by the standard-setter — effectively permitting grandfathering of pre-existing accounting policies (<ParaRef std="IFRS 6" para="6-7" />). The entity's chosen policy must still meet the relevance and reliability criteria of NAS 8 (<ParaRef std="IFRS 6" para="9" />), but the NAS 8 hierarchy for selecting accounting policies (analogous NFRS, Framework definitions, other standard-setters) need not be applied to E&E expenditures (<ParaRef std="IFRS 6" para="6" />). This is a significant scope carve-out from the normal policy selection framework.

The impairment model under NFRS 6 modifies NAS 36 in two important ways: (a) the standard specifies its own list of impairment indicators rather than applying NAS 36's general indicators (<ParaRef std="IFRS 6" para="18-20" />), including expiry of exploration rights, abandonment plans, and evidence that the carrying amount is unlikely to be recovered through successful development or sale; and (b) it permits aggregation of E&E assets into larger units for impairment testing than would normally be permitted under NAS 36's cash-generating unit concept (<ParaRef std="IFRS 6" para="21-22" />).

Nepal's limited but evolving mining sector presents unique challenges: (a) exploration licenses issued by the Department of Mines and Geology have defined terms and conditions — expiry without renewal is a clear impairment indicator; (b) environmental regulations and community resistance (particularly in hill and mountain districts) can render technically viable deposits commercially unviable; (c) the transition from exploration to development is poorly defined for small-scale mining operations common in Nepal — entities must exercise judgment about when "technical feasibility and commercial viability" are demonstrable for operations that may never reach the scale of international mining projects.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NFRS 6 applies only during the **exploration and evaluation phase** — the period after a company gets permission to explore a specific area and before it confirms that extracting the minerals is technically feasible and commercially worthwhile. It does **not** cover costs incurred before obtaining exploration rights (those are expensed) or costs incurred after confirming a viable deposit (those are covered by other standards like NAS 16 and NAS 38).

**Key terms:**
- **Exploration and evaluation expenditures** — Costs incurred searching for mineral resources after obtaining legal exploration rights but before proving technical feasibility and commercial viability.
- **Exploration and evaluation assets** — E&E expenditures that are capitalised (recorded as assets) under the entity's chosen accounting policy.
- **Mineral resources** — Minerals, oil, natural gas, and similar non-regenerative resources.
- **Technical feasibility** — Confirmation that extracting the resource is technically possible with available technology.
- **Commercial viability** — Confirmation that extraction will be economically profitable.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 6 has a narrow scope window: it applies only to expenditures incurred in the E&E phase (<ParaRef std="IFRS 6" para="3-5" />). Pre-exploration costs (e.g., general geological surveys before obtaining a license) are outside scope and are expensed under normal standards. Post-E&E development costs (after technical feasibility and commercial viability are demonstrated) exit NFRS 6 and enter NAS 16 or NAS 38.

Key technical considerations:
- **Classification** — E&E assets may be classified as tangible (drilling equipment, trenching) or intangible (drilling rights, geological data, exploratory surveys) depending on their nature. The classification determines subsequent accounting: tangible E&E assets follow NAS 16 measurement principles, intangible E&E assets follow NAS 38 (<ParaRef std="IFRS 6" para="15-17" />).
- **Examples of E&E expenditures** — Acquisition of rights to explore; topographical, geological, geochemical, and geophysical studies; exploratory drilling; trenching; sampling; activities related to evaluating technical feasibility and commercial viability (<ParaRef std="IFRS 6" para="9" />).
- **Excluded costs** — Development expenditures post-feasibility, general administrative overheads not directly attributable to E&E activities, and costs incurred before obtaining exploration rights.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope boundary between "exploration and evaluation" and "development" is the most significant judgment area. NFRS 6 does not define when E&E ends and development begins — it states only that this occurs when "technical feasibility and commercial viability of extracting a mineral resource are demonstrable" (<ParaRef std="IFRS 6" para="5" />). For large-scale international mining, this is typically linked to a bankable feasibility study. For Nepal's smaller-scale operations — a limestone quarry for a cement plant or a dimension stone operation — the threshold is less clear.

The interaction between NFRS 6 and the government licensing regime creates a natural scope boundary in Nepal: the Department of Mines and Geology issues exploration licenses (under which NFRS 6 applies) and mining licenses (under which development-phase accounting under NAS 16/38 begins). However, entities may obtain mining licenses before completing all E&E activities, creating an overlap period requiring judgment.

Scope interactions with other standards: (a) NAS 38's general prohibition on capitalising internally generated intangible assets does not apply to E&E intangibles because NFRS 6 provides a specific exemption; (b) NFRS 6 does not exempt entities from the Framework's asset definition — the entity must still believe future economic benefits are probable for capitalisation; (c) environmental rehabilitation obligations associated with exploration activities are within NAS 37's scope, not NFRS 6 — they are recognised as provisions when the constructive or legal obligation arises.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

NFRS 6 permits entities to develop their own accounting policy for the recognition of E&E expenditures. An entity may capitalise E&E expenditures as assets or expense them as incurred — the key requirement is consistency. Once the entity establishes its policy, it must apply it consistently to similar E&E activities. When capitalised, E&E assets must be tested for impairment when indicators exist, and must be reclassified out of E&E when technical feasibility and commercial viability are demonstrated.

In Nepal, cement companies exploring new limestone deposits typically capitalise geological survey costs, core drilling costs, and license fees as exploration assets. These are reclassified to NAS 16 tangible assets (quarry development costs) once the deposit is proven and a mining license is obtained. Smaller operators — particularly sand and aggregate businesses — typically expense E&E costs as incurred because the amounts are relatively small and the exploration phase is short.

## Measurement

E&E assets are initially measured at cost. After recognition, the entity applies either the cost model or the revaluation model (consistent with NAS 16 for tangible E&E assets or NAS 38 for intangible E&E assets). The impairment test under NFRS 6 is triggered when specific indicators are present — not routinely at each reporting date. Indicators include: the exploration license has expired or will expire soon with no renewal planned; substantive expenditure on further E&E is not planned; exploration has not led to discovery of commercially viable quantities; and sufficient data exists to conclude that the carrying amount is unlikely to be recovered.

In Nepal, the most common impairment trigger is license expiry without renewal or abandonment of exploration areas. Cement companies that explored limestone deposits in multiple locations may abandon some sites after geological analysis reveals insufficient reserves or excessive overburden ratios. The impairment loss is measured under NAS 36 — the carrying amount of the E&E asset is compared with its recoverable amount (higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use).

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

NFRS 6 requires entities to classify E&E assets as tangible or intangible according to their nature and to apply that classification consistently. Reclassification from E&E to another asset category occurs when technical feasibility and commercial viability are demonstrated — at that point, the entity tests for impairment under NFRS 6 before reclassifying.

Key disclosures include: the accounting policies applied to E&E expenditures (capitalisation vs. expensing), the amounts of assets, liabilities, income, expense, and operating and investing cash flows arising from E&E activities, and the basis for any impairment assessment. In Nepal, entities with E&E activities should disclose the nature and location of exploration areas, the status of licenses (including expiry dates), and any environmental obligations associated with exploration activities. Given the limited application of NFRS 6 in Nepal, many entities have no E&E disclosures — but those in the cement, mining, and potential hydrocarbon sectors must carefully evaluate whether their activities fall within the standard's scope.
