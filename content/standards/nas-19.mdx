## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

When you work for a company, you earn more than just your monthly salary. Your employer might provide a Dashain bonus, contribute to your provident fund, promise you gratuity when you leave, or offer medical insurance. All of these are **employee benefits** — and NAS 19 tells companies how to account for them.

Think of it like a piggy bank your employer fills on your behalf. Some benefits are simple: your salary is paid this month for this month's work — easy. But other benefits are like a promise for the future: "Work here for 5 years and we will give you gratuity." The company needs to set aside money gradually, not wait until you leave and then scramble to pay.

In Nepal, the **Labour Act 2074** requires employers to pay gratuity of **30 days' salary for each year of service** after an employee completes one year of continuous service. This is a defined benefit — the amount depends on your final salary, not on how much was contributed to a fund. NAS 19 requires companies to estimate the total future gratuity cost using actuarial calculations and recognise the liability over the employee's service period. Additionally, employers must contribute to the **Employees' Provident Fund (EPF)** — 10% from employee and 10% from employer — which is a defined contribution plan (simpler, since the company's obligation is limited to the contributions made).

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 19 prescribes the accounting treatment for all forms of employee benefits except share-based payments (covered by NFRS 2). The standard categorises employee benefits into four groups: **short-term benefits** (expected to be settled within 12 months — salaries, bonuses, paid leave, medical benefits); **post-employment benefits** (pensions, gratuity, post-employment medical care); **other long-term benefits** (long-service leave, jubilee bonuses, deferred compensation); and **termination benefits** (redundancy payments, early retirement inducements).

The most complex area is **defined benefit plans**, where the employer's obligation is to provide the agreed benefit — the actuarial and investment risk falls on the employer. Nepal's statutory gratuity under Labour Act 2074 (30 days' salary per completed year of service) is a defined benefit obligation because the benefit depends on final salary and length of service. NAS 19 requires this to be measured using the Projected Unit Credit method with actuarial assumptions (discount rate, salary escalation, mortality, employee turnover).

**Defined contribution plans** are simpler: the employer's obligation is limited to the amount contributed. Nepal's Employees' Provident Fund (EPF), where both employer and employee contribute 10% of basic salary, is a defined contribution plan — once the entity pays its 10%, it has no further obligation regardless of how the fund performs.

Actuarial gains and losses on defined benefit plans (called **remeasurements**) are recognised in Other Comprehensive Income (OCI) and are never reclassified to profit or loss. This was a major change from the previous "corridor" approach.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 19 implements a balance-sheet-driven model for defined benefit plans: the net defined benefit liability (or asset) is the deficit (or surplus) of the plan measured as the difference between the present value of the defined benefit obligation (DBO) and the fair value of plan assets (<ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="63" />). The DBO is measured using the Projected Unit Credit method (<ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="67" />), which attributes benefit to periods of service using the plan's benefit formula and discounts using high-quality corporate bond yields — or government bond yields where no deep corporate bond market exists.

Nepal presents a significant challenge: there is no deep market for high-quality corporate bonds. Practitioners therefore use Nepal Government Bond yields as the discount rate, consistent with <ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="83" />. The yield curve is limited — NRB auctions development bonds of varying tenors, but liquidity beyond 10 years is thin. Actuaries must extrapolate, and the choice of discount rate materially impacts the DBO for long-service employees.

The scarcity of qualified actuaries in Nepal is a practical barrier to compliance. Unlike India (with thousands of practising actuaries), Nepal has very few. Many entities rely on Indian actuarial firms or use simplified calculations that may not fully comply with <ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="75-98" /> actuarial assumption requirements. Assumptions that matter most in the Nepali context: salary escalation rate (8-12% for mid-career employees in banking), employee turnover (high in private sector, low in government-linked entities), and retirement age (58 years for most private sector, 60 for banking under NRB norms).

The asset ceiling test (<ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="64" />) limits the net defined benefit asset to the present value of economic benefits available as refunds or reductions in future contributions. In Nepal, where gratuity is typically unfunded (no separate plan assets), the test is rarely relevant — the entity has a net liability, not a net asset.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope &amp; key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 19 applies to **all employee benefits** except share-based payments (like stock options, covered by NFRS 2).

**Key terms:**
- **Employee benefits** — All forms of consideration given by an entity in exchange for service rendered by employees.
- **Defined contribution plan** — The employer pays a fixed amount into a fund. The employee bears the investment risk. Example: Nepal's Employees' Provident Fund (EPF) — employer pays 10%, obligation ends there.
- **Defined benefit plan** — The employer promises a specific benefit amount. The employer bears the risk. Example: Nepal's statutory gratuity — 30 days' salary per year of service.
- **Gratuity** — A lump-sum payment made to employees upon leaving, based on salary and years of service.
- **Actuarial valuation** — A mathematical calculation using assumptions about future salary increases, employee turnover, and discount rates to estimate the current cost of future benefits.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 19 applies to all employee benefits including those provided through formal plans, legislation (Labour Act 2074), industry practices, and informal practices that create constructive obligations (<ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="4" />). Share-based payments (NFRS 2) are excluded.

Key technical definitions:
- **Defined benefit obligation (DBO)** — The present value of expected future payments required to settle the obligation from employee service in current and prior periods, measured without deducting plan assets.
- **Plan assets** — Assets held by a long-term employee benefit fund, plus qualifying insurance policies (<ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="8" />). In Nepal, gratuity is typically unfunded (no separate plan assets), making the DBO equal to the net liability.
- **Service cost** — Comprises current service cost (benefit attributed to current period), past service cost (change in DBO from plan amendment or curtailment), and any gain or loss on settlement.
- **Net interest** — The change during the period in the net defined benefit liability resulting from the passage of time, calculated by applying the discount rate to the net liability.
- **Remeasurements** — Actuarial gains and losses, return on plan assets (excluding net interest), and changes in the asset ceiling. Recognised in OCI, never reclassified to P&L.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

The scope interaction with NFRS 2 (Share-based Payment) is clear in principle but complex in practice when entities offer hybrid compensation packages combining cash bonuses (NAS 19) with phantom share schemes settled in cash (potentially NFRS 2 if equity-linked). Multi-employer plans (<ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="32-39" />) are rare in Nepal but arise in group structures where a parent company's gratuity fund covers subsidiaries.

The constructive obligation concept (<ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="4" />) extends NAS 19 beyond legal requirements. If a Nepali bank has consistently paid Dashain bonuses equal to one month's salary for 20 years without a contractual obligation, NAS 19 requires recognition of a liability because the past practice creates a constructive obligation — employees have a valid expectation. This is separate from the legal obligation under Labour Act 2074.

Edge case: the Labour Act 2074 changed the gratuity formula from 20 days' salary per year (under the old Labour Act 2048) to 30 days' salary per year. For entities transitioning, this amendment constitutes a plan amendment under <ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="101-112" />, generating past service cost recognised immediately in profit or loss.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

Short-term employee benefits are recognised as an expense when the employee renders service, with any unpaid amount recognised as a liability. Accumulated compensated absences (e.g., unused annual leave that can be carried forward) are recognised when employees render service that increases their entitlement.

For defined contribution plans, the entity recognises a contribution payable as an expense in the period the employee renders service (<ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="51" />). Nepal's EPF contributions of 10% employer share are expensed monthly.

For defined benefit plans, the entity recognises: (a) service cost in profit or loss; (b) net interest on the net defined benefit liability in profit or loss; and (c) remeasurements (actuarial gains and losses) in OCI (<ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="120" />). The net defined benefit liability on the balance sheet equals the present value of the DBO minus the fair value of plan assets (if any). Since most Nepali entities do not fund their gratuity obligations through separate trusts, the entire DBO appears as a liability.

Termination benefits are recognised at the earlier of when the entity can no longer withdraw the offer and when it recognises related restructuring costs under NAS 37 (<ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="165" />).

## Measurement

The DBO is measured using the Projected Unit Credit method, which attributes units of benefit to each period of service and discounts the total estimated future benefit using a rate based on high-quality corporate bonds (or government bonds where no deep corporate bond market exists). Key actuarial assumptions for Nepal include: discount rate (Nepal Government Bond yield, typically 7-9%), salary escalation rate (8-12%), employee turnover (varies by sector — low in banking at 3-5%, higher in hospitality at 15-20%), and retirement age (58 for most private sector entities).

Current service cost and past service cost are measured using the same actuarial assumptions as the DBO. Net interest is calculated by multiplying the discount rate by the net defined benefit liability at the start of the period. Remeasurements — the difference between actual and expected outcomes — include actuarial gains and losses from changes in demographic and financial assumptions.

## Presentation &amp; disclosure

The DBO net of plan assets is presented as a non-current liability (or asset) in the statement of financial position. Service cost and net interest are presented in profit or loss; remeasurements in OCI. Key disclosures include: the characteristics and risks of the plan, explanation of amounts in the financial statements, sensitivity analysis for each significant actuarial assumption, and a description of asset-liability matching strategies (<ParaRef std="IAS 19" para="135-152" />).

In Nepal, most entities disclose gratuity as "Provision for Staff Gratuity" or "Employee Benefit Obligation." NRB requires BFIs to disclose staff-related provisions separately, including gratuity, leave encashment, and provident fund contributions. Entities must also disclose the name of the actuary and the date of the most recent actuarial valuation — a disclosure many smaller entities omit, particularly when using internal calculations rather than independent actuarial reports.
