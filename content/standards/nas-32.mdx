## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

When a company raises money, it can issue shares (equity) or borrow (liability). The distinction sounds simple, but it gets tricky with instruments like preference shares and convertible debentures. NAS 32 provides rules for deciding whether a financial instrument should appear as a "liability" on the balance sheet (money you owe) or as "equity" (money that belongs to shareholders).

Consider this: if Himalayan Bank issues preference shares that pay a fixed 8% dividend and must be redeemed (bought back) after 5 years, are these really "shares" or are they more like a loan? NAS 32 says: look at the substance, not the legal form. Since the bank is obligated to pay cash (dividends and redemption), these preference shares are classified as a **liability**, not equity — even though they are legally called "shares."

**Why does this matter?** Wrong classification inflates equity, misleads investors, and distorts financial ratios like debt-to-equity. For NEPSE-listed companies, this directly affects how investors evaluate the company's financial health.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 32 establishes the principles for classifying financial instruments from the issuer's perspective as financial liabilities or equity instruments. The fundamental principle is substance over form: the classification is driven by the contractual obligation, not the legal label. An instrument is a financial liability if it contains a contractual obligation to deliver cash or another financial asset, or to exchange financial instruments under potentially unfavourable conditions. An instrument is equity only if it evidences a residual interest in the entity's net assets with no obligation to deliver cash.

This distinction is critical for Nepal's BFI sector. Preference shares — common in promoter capital structures — may be equity or liability depending on their terms. Convertible debentures listed on NEPSE are compound instruments requiring bifurcation into a liability component and an equity component. NRB's capital adequacy framework references NAS 32 classification: only instruments classified as equity under NAS 32 can count toward regulatory capital.

NAS 32 also covers offsetting: a financial asset and a financial liability may be offset only when the entity has a legally enforceable right to set off and intends either to settle net or to realise the asset and settle the liability simultaneously.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NAS 32 operationalises the liability-equity distinction through the "obligation to deliver cash" test (<ParaRef std="IAS 32" para="15–20" />). The standard's architecture creates a binary classification with no intermediate category — an instrument is either a liability or equity. This bright-line approach creates economically significant classification consequences: liability classification means distributions are interest expense (P&L), while equity classification means distributions are dividends (equity movement).

The compound instrument provisions (<ParaRef std="IAS 32" para="28–32" />) require bifurcation when an instrument contains both a liability component and an equity component. The liability component is measured first at fair value (present value of contractual cash flows discounted at market rate for a similar instrument without the conversion feature), with the residual allocated to equity. This is directly relevant for convertible debentures issued by Nepali corporates listed on NEPSE — the equity component is typically small relative to the liability component, but its identification is mandatory.

Treasury share provisions (<ParaRef std="IAS 32" para="33–34" />) require own equity instruments reacquired to be deducted from equity, not held as assets. Nepal's promoter share buyback transactions and BFI share repurchase programmes must follow this treatment. The puttable instrument exception (<ParaRef std="IAS 32" para="16A–16D" />) — allowing equity classification for certain puttable instruments meeting strict criteria — has limited relevance in Nepal but applies to cooperative shares and certain mutual fund structures.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope & key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NAS 32 covers any financial instrument — a contract that creates a financial asset for one party and a financial liability or equity instrument for the other party.

**What it covers:**
- Ordinary shares, preference shares, debentures, bonds
- Convertible instruments (instruments that can be converted from one type to another)
- Treasury shares (a company buying back its own shares)
- Offsetting of financial assets and liabilities

**Not covered:** Recognition and measurement (NFRS 9) and disclosure (NFRS 7) are dealt with by other standards.

**Key terms:**
- **Financial liability** — An obligation to deliver cash or another financial asset. Like a loan — you must pay it back.
- **Equity instrument** — A residual interest in the entity's assets after deducting all liabilities. Like ordinary shares — shareholders get what's left.
- **Compound instrument** — An instrument with both liability and equity features, like a convertible debenture.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NAS 32 applies to the classification of financial instruments by the issuer into financial assets, financial liabilities, and equity instruments; the classification of related interest, dividends, losses, and gains; and the circumstances for offsetting (<ParaRef std="IAS 32" para="2–3" />).

Key definitions:
- **Financial instrument** — a contract giving rise to a financial asset of one entity and a financial liability or equity instrument of another.
- **Financial liability** — a contractual obligation to deliver cash or another financial asset, or to exchange financial instruments under potentially unfavourable conditions.
- **Equity instrument** — any contract evidencing a residual interest in the assets of an entity after deducting all its liabilities.
- **Compound instrument** — a non-derivative instrument containing both a liability and an equity component (e.g., convertible debenture).
- **Treasury shares** — own equity instruments reacquired by the entity or its subsidiaries.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

Scope boundary cases in Nepal: (a) insurance contracts within the scope of NFRS 17 are excluded from NAS 32 unless they meet the definition of a financial guarantee contract (<ParaRef std="IAS 32" para="4(d)" />), (b) employee share options and other equity-settled share-based payments are within NFRS 2's scope for measurement but NAS 32 governs their classification as equity, (c) contingent consideration in a business combination — classified as liability or equity under NAS 32 at acquisition date, with subsequent measurement depending on classification, (d) the "fixed-for-fixed" criterion (<ParaRef std="IAS 32" para="22" />) for derivatives on own equity is critical: only derivatives settled by exchanging a fixed amount of cash for a fixed number of own equity instruments qualify as equity. NRB's mandatory convertible bond requirements for BFIs must be assessed under this criterion.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

Classification is determined at initial recognition based on the substance of the contractual arrangement, not its legal form (<ParaRef std="IAS 32" para="15" />). Once classified, the classification is not subsequently changed unless the instrument is modified. For compound instruments, the entity allocates the initial carrying amount between the liability and equity components at inception: the liability component is measured first at fair value, and the equity component is the residual (<ParaRef std="IAS 32" para="31–32" />). No gain or loss arises from initial classification or allocation.

## Measurement

NAS 32 is primarily a classification and presentation standard — measurement is governed by NFRS 9. However, the standard specifies the initial allocation methodology for compound instruments. The liability component is measured at the fair value of a similar liability without an equity conversion feature (typically using a discounted cash flow model at the market interest rate for an equivalent non-convertible instrument). The equity component equals the total proceeds minus the liability component's fair value. Transaction costs are allocated proportionally between the components. In Nepal, the discount rate for bifurcating convertible debentures should reflect the credit risk of the issuer — BFI debentures typically use NRB-published reference rates plus a credit spread.

## Presentation & disclosure

Financial liabilities and equity instruments must be classified on the face of the balance sheet according to their substance. Interest, dividends, and gains/losses follow the classification: dividends on instruments classified as liabilities are presented as interest expense in profit or loss; dividends on equity instruments are recognised directly in equity (<ParaRef std="IAS 32" para="35–41" />). Offsetting is permitted only when the entity has a legally enforceable right of set-off and intends to settle net or simultaneously (<ParaRef std="IAS 32" para="42–50" />). Detailed disclosures of terms, conditions, and accounting policies are required under NFRS 7, which complements NAS 32's presentation requirements.
