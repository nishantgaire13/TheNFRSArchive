## Overview

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

Think of revenue as the money a business earns by selling goods or providing services. But when exactly should a business record that money in its books? That is what NFRS 15 answers.

Before this standard, Nepal had different rules for different types of sales — one for construction projects, another for regular sales. NFRS 15 replaces all of that with one simple five-step process. The basic idea: you record revenue when you actually deliver what you promised to the customer.

**Why does this matter?** If a company records revenue too early (before delivering), its profits look bigger than they really are. If it records too late, profits look smaller. NFRS 15 ensures everyone follows the same timing rules.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

NFRS 15 establishes a single, comprehensive framework for recognising revenue. It replaces a patchwork of earlier standards — NAS 11 (Construction Contracts) and NAS 18 (Revenue) — with one unified five-step model. The core idea: an entity recognises revenue when it transfers a promised good or service to the customer, in an amount that reflects the consideration it expects to receive.

For Nepali preparers, this standard governs everything from straightforward product sales to multi-element telecom bundles and long-term construction contracts. It matters because Nepal's adoption brought local practice in line with global IFRS reporting for the first time across the entire revenue cycle.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

NFRS 15 codifies a single asset-liability model for revenue, replacing the risks-and-rewards framework of NAS 18 and NAS 11 with a control-transfer model. The standard's unit of account is the performance obligation — not the contract — which forces disaggregation of bundled arrangements that legacy standards treated as single deliverables.

The shift is architecturally significant: revenue is now a consequence of changes in contract assets/liabilities, not a standalone income-statement event. This aligns revenue recognition with the Conceptual Framework's balance-sheet primacy. Nepal adopted verbatim from the 2018 IFRS Blue Book; the three-year delayed effective date (1 Shrawan 2078 BS) allowed dual-reporting during transition but no substantive local divergence.

Key tension in practice: the five-step model is principles-based, but the 70+ pages of application guidance (IFRS 15.B1–B89) create de facto rules. Nepali preparers often under-utilise this guidance, leading to inconsistent application across sectors.

</DifficultyContent>

## Scope & key definitions

<DifficultyContent level="beginner">

NFRS 15 applies whenever a business sells something to a customer. It does not apply to leases (that is NFRS 16), insurance (NFRS 17), or financial investments (NFRS 9).

**Three key terms to understand:**

- **Contract** — An agreement between a buyer and seller. Even a verbal agreement counts if both sides have clear obligations.
- **Performance obligation** — A promise to deliver something. If you sell a phone with a warranty, that is two promises: the phone and the warranty service.
- **Transaction price** — The total amount you expect to receive from the customer. If there are discounts or bonuses, you adjust this amount.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="medium">

The standard applies to all contracts with customers, with narrow exceptions for leases (NFRS 16), insurance contracts (NFRS 17), and financial instruments (NFRS 9). A **contract** must create enforceable rights and obligations — verbal agreements count if they meet the criteria in <ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="9" />.

Key terms that matter in practice:

- **Performance obligation** — a distinct promise to transfer a good or service. Identifying these correctly is where most preparers trip up.
- **Transaction price** — the amount an entity expects to receive, adjusted for variable consideration, time value, and non-cash consideration.
- **Stand-alone selling price** — used to allocate the transaction price when a contract has multiple performance obligations.

</DifficultyContent>

<DifficultyContent level="pro">

Scope exclusions merit careful boundary analysis. NFRS 15 governs the "customer" side of a transaction — the counterparty must be purchasing outputs of the entity's ordinary activities, not collaborating in an activity that shares risks and rewards (which would be a joint arrangement under NFRS 11). The non-monetary exchange exclusion (<ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="6" />) catches barter transactions between entities in the same line of business (common in Nepal's media and telecom sectors).

The definition of "distinct" at <ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="27" /> operates on two axes: (a) the customer can benefit from the good/service on its own or with readily available resources, AND (b) the promise is separately identifiable from other promises in the contract. Criterion (b) is the harder test — integration, modification, and high interdependence all negate separability.

Stand-alone selling price estimation methods — adjusted market assessment, expected cost plus margin, and residual approach — are hierarchically ordered in practice. The residual approach is a last resort requiring observable evidence for all other obligations (<ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="79" />). Nepali entities frequently default to it without justification.

</DifficultyContent>

## Recognition

Revenue recognition follows five steps, applied in sequence (<ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="IN7" />):

1. **Identify the contract** — confirm it meets the five criteria (approval, identifiable rights, payment terms, commercial substance, collectability).
2. **Identify performance obligations** — separate each distinct promise. A good or service is distinct if the customer can benefit from it on its own and it is separately identifiable within the contract (<ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="27" />).
3. **Determine the transaction price** — account for variable consideration, constraining estimates to amounts highly probable of not reversing (<ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="56" />).
4. **Allocate the transaction price** — pro-rata based on stand-alone selling prices. Use observable prices where available; otherwise estimate (<ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="76" />).
5. **Recognise revenue** — when (or as) the entity satisfies a performance obligation by transferring control. Control transfers either at a point in time or over time (<ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="31" />).

The practical takeaway: if you can identify your performance obligations correctly, the rest of the model follows logically. That first separation step is where audit adjustments cluster.

## Measurement

Revenue is measured at the transaction price allocated to each satisfied performance obligation. Variable consideration — discounts, rebates, penalties, performance bonuses — is included only to the extent that a significant reversal is not highly probable (<ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="56" />).

When a contract has a significant financing component (more than one year between payment and delivery), the transaction price is adjusted for the time value of money (<ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="60" />). In Nepal, this commonly affects construction contracts with milestone billing and long-term equipment supply agreements.

Contract costs that give rise to an asset (costs to obtain or fulfil a contract) are capitalised and amortised on a systematic basis consistent with the pattern of revenue recognition (<ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="91" />).

## Presentation & disclosure

On the face of the statement of financial position, an entity presents a **contract asset** (when performance exceeds billing) or a **contract liability** (when billing exceeds performance). These replace the old "amounts due from/to customers" terminology.

The disclosure requirements are extensive. Preparers must disaggregate revenue into categories that depict how the nature, amount, timing, and uncertainty of revenue are affected by economic factors (<ParaRef std="IFRS 15" para="114" />). Typical disaggregation dimensions for Nepali entities include: product type, geography (domestic vs. export), timing of transfer (point in time vs. over time), and contract duration.

Qualitative and quantitative information about performance obligations, significant judgements, and assets from contract costs round out the note disclosures.
